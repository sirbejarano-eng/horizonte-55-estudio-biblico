Daniel at the court of Babylon

Daniel tells how a young man from Judah, taken captive to Babylon, is trained at the king’s court without ceasing to be faithful to his God, and how, years later, he interprets the writing on the wall on the night the kingdom falls. We will first observe the sacred text and then Nebuchadnezzar’s Babylon, the Belshazzar of the inscriptions and the debates that remain open.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what Babylon and its inscriptions contribute and what remains open, such as the date of the book and the identity of Darius the Mede.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Ancient context helps when three things are kept apart: the Babylon that can be studied, the inscriptions that shed light on its kings and the questions that are still debated.

Widely recognized context

Nebuchadnezzar’s Babylon

Nebuchadnezzar II (604–562 BC) built the Ishtar Gate, now reconstructed in Berlin with glazed bricks; his cylinders and the bricks stamped with his name record his building works in Babylon.

Widely recognized context

Belshazzar, son of Nabonidus

For centuries Belshazzar was known by name only from Daniel; Xenophon related the death of a king’s son without naming him Belshazzar. Since 1854, cuneiform inscriptions—including cylinders of Nabonidus found at Ur—have documented him as the king’s son, and other texts show that he governed in Babylon during his father’s absence.

Widely recognized context

A book in two languages

Daniel is written in Hebrew (1:1–2:4a and chapters 8–12) and in Aramaic (2:4b–7:28), the language in which the Chaldeans speak to the king in Daniel 2:4.

Unproven identification

The date of composition

Jewish and Christian tradition ascribes the book to Daniel in the sixth century BC; many scholars date it around 167–164 BC, during the persecution under Antiochus IV. Both positions are argued; the question remains open.

Unproven identification

Darius the Mede

No document outside the Bible, except Josephus, mentions him. Some regard him as an error or a confusion with Darius I; others propose identifying him with Gubaru, with Cyaxares or with Cyrus himself. There is no consensus.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its context

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: faithful in a foreign land

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that even in exile God remains the Lord of history: “He removes kings, and sets up kings” (Daniel 2:21). Daniel learns the language and serves at court, but does not surrender his loyalty: he “purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself” (1:8).

Which details it helps explain

“The Lord gave” Jehoiakim (1:2) and the same God gives the young men wisdom (1:17); Nebuchadnezzar opens the book and Belshazzar is “weighed in the balances” (5:27). The pride that profanes the temple vessels (5:2–4, 23) contrasts with Daniel’s humility.

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself settle the date of the book’s composition or the identity of every king it mentions.

02

Nebuchadnezzar’s Babylon

What it contributes

It helps us picture the city Daniel came to. Nebuchadnezzar II raised the Ishtar Gate, covered with blue glazed bricks and animals in relief, and left his name stamped on bricks and on clay cylinders that describe his temples, ziggurats, palaces and fortifications in Babylon and Borsippa.

Which details it helps explain

Hundreds of thousands of fragments of the gate were taken to Berlin, where it was reconstructed in the Pergamon Museum along with part of the Processional Way. The cylinders and bricks are written in Akkadian cuneiform; Daniel 1:4 speaks of “the learning and the language of the Chaldeans”, and 2:4 mentions Aramaic, the common language of the empire.

Which limit must remain

These monuments show the grandeur of Nebuchadnezzar’s Babylon, but they do not name Daniel or his companions: they illustrate the setting, they do not prove the account.

03

Belshazzar, Nabonidus and the debates about the book

What it contributes

It explains a detail that was a puzzle for centuries. Known sources gave Nabonidus as the last king of Babylon; the Ur cylinders include a prayer for “Belshazzar, his son”, and other texts show that Nabonidus entrusted him with the government during his long absence. That is why it makes sense for Belshazzar to offer Daniel the “third” place in the kingdom (5:7, 16, 29).

Which details it helps explain

Nabonidus reigned from 555 to 539 BC, and Babylon fell to the Persians in October 539. Babylonian texts do not give Belshazzar the title of king, although he wielded power. On the date of the book, many scholars place it in 167–164 BC and see Darius the Mede as a figure confused with Darius I; others, such as Tom Finley, defend Daniel’s authorship and propose, following D. J. Wiseman, that “Darius” would be another name for Cyrus (6:28).

Which limit must remain

That Belshazzar is documented shows accurate knowledge of late Babylon, but it does not by itself decide the date of the book or resolve the identity of Darius the Mede.

What you can now recognize

What context contributes to the account of Daniel

Nebuchadnezzar’s Babylon, with its gate of glazed bricks and its royal inscriptions, and the Belshazzar who reappeared in the Nabonidus cylinders help place the account in a real world. But they do not name Daniel, fix the date of the book or resolve who Darius the Mede was.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he hands over and sustains, gives wisdom to the faithful and judges the arrogance of kings. Context illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

Daniel “purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself”, but he asks respectfully and proposes a ten-day test. The text attributes the outcome to God: he gave Daniel favour with the prince of the eunuchs (1:9) and gave the young men knowledge and skill (1:17).

2. Who removes and sets up kings?

In Daniel 2:20–22, what does Daniel confess about God, and how does it shed light on Belshazzar’s fall in chapter 5?

Daniel blesses God because wisdom and might are his, and because “he removes kings, and sets up kings”. Belshazzar’s night shows that confession in history: the kingdom that seemed secure is “weighed in the balances” and passes to others (5:26–28).

Key terms

Brief glossary

Chaldeans
People of southern Mesopotamia from whom Nebuchadnezzar’s dynasty came; in Daniel the term also refers to the court’s wise men.
Ishtar Gate
Monumental gate of Babylon built by Nebuchadnezzar II, with glazed bricks and animals in relief; now reconstructed in Berlin.
Foundation cylinder
Clay cylinder with a cuneiform inscription that Mesopotamian kings placed in their buildings.
Nabonidus
Last king of Babylon (555–539 BC) and father of Belshazzar, to whom he entrusted the government during his absence.
Aramaic
Semitic language common across the empire; the central part of Daniel (2:4b–7:28) is written in it.
