Paul in Athens: the Areopagus

Acts 17 tells of Paul’s brief stay in Athens: the dialogue in the synagogue and the agora, the encounter with Epicureans and Stoics and the speech at the Areopagus. We will first observe the sacred text and then the first-century city, its philosophical schools and the question of exactly where Paul spoke. The study on Paul’s journeys provides the wider setting of this episode.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what the history and archaeology of Athens contribute and what remains a proposal about the place and the altar.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

The sacred text says that Paul was brought “to the Areopagus” and spoke “in the middle of the Areopagus”, but it does not make clear whether this means the hill or the council that bore its name.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

First-century Athens is a well-known city; even so, the monuments and ancient texts that can be studied, the proposals about where the speech took place and the message narrated by Scripture should be kept apart.

Widely recognized context

A city that can be walked today

Above Athens rose the Acropolis with the Parthenon (447–432 BC). In the agora, the Stoa of Attalos, built by Attalus II in the second century BC, was rebuilt in 1953–56 and today houses the Agora museum.

Widely recognized context

Philosophers with schools in Athens

Epicureans and Stoics were Athenian schools: Epicurus taught in his Garden, and Zeno founded Stoicism in the Stoa Poikile, in the agora, in the early third century BC.

Widely recognized context

“We are also his offspring”

The phrase in Acts 17:28 comes from the opening of the Phaenomena of Aratus of Soli (about 315–245 BC), a poem widely read in antiquity and Stoic in tone.

Unproven identification

The hill or the council?

The Areopagus was both a hill beside the Acropolis and the council that met there. Some think Paul spoke on the hill; others that he appeared before the council, which in the first century may also have met in the Stoa Basileios in the agora.

Unproven identification

The specific altar

Ancient writers such as Pausanias mention altars in Athens “of the gods named Unknown”, but the altar Paul cites has not been found. Nor is there agreement on whether “in him we live, move, and have our being” quotes Epimenides.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its context

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: the Creator God who calls to repentance

What it contributes

It presents the God who made the world, gives all people life and breath and is not far from anyone; he calls everyone to repent and has given proof of his judgement by raising Jesus (Acts 17:24–31).

Which details it helps explain

Paul starts from what his hearers know — an altar, a Greek poet — without diluting the message: the true God does not dwell in temples or take the form of gold, silver or stone (17:24, 29), and the resurrection provokes both mockery and faith (17:32–34).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix where Paul spoke or which altar he saw.

02

First-century Athens under Rome

What it contributes

It places the account in a city conquered by Sulla in 86 BC but respected by Rome, with its classical monuments, its markets and an intellectual prestige that was still alive.

Which details it helps explain

The Acropolis and the Parthenon dominated the city; in the agora stood the Stoa of Attalos, rebuilt today, and the Stoa Poikile of the Stoics. Some think Paul’s daily dialogue (Acts 17:17) took place in the Roman market, built with help from Caesar and Augustus. The Epicureans taught that the gods pay no attention to human beings; the Stoics, that a divine logos orders the cosmos, and Aratus, close to them, had written “we are also his offspring”.

Which limit must remain

Knowing the city and its philosophies illuminates the setting, but it cannot tell us what each listener said or reconstruct the dialogue beyond the text.

03

The Areopagus: hill, council and altars

What it contributes

It explains why the word “Areopagus” is ambiguous: it names the hill of Ares, northwest of the Acropolis, and the ancient council that met on it and that in the Roman period regained administrative, religious and educational functions.

Which details it helps explain

One reading places the speech on the hill, where a bronze plaque today reproduces the text; another, before the council, perhaps meeting in the Stoa Basileios in the agora. Pausanias (second century AD) mentions altars “of the gods named Unknown” at the port of Phalerum, and Philostratus also speaks of altars to unknown gods in Athens. Dionysius, one of those who believed, was a member of the council (17:34).

Which limit must remain

Ancient texts show that such altars existed, but they do not identify the one Paul saw; and neither the hill nor the Stoa Basileios can be pointed to with certainty as the place of the speech.

What you can now recognize

What Athens contributes to the Areopagus speech

The Acropolis, the agora with its stoas and the schools of the Epicureans and Stoics are well documented, and the quotation from Aratus shows that Paul knew the poetry of his hearers. This gives historical solidity to the account in Acts. But the exact place of the speech and the specific altar remain open questions.

The centre of the account lies in the proclamation: the God who created the world does not dwell in temples, is not far from anyone and calls everyone to repentance through the risen Jesus. Athens illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Areopagus
“Hill of Ares”, northwest of the Acropolis, and the name of the ancient Athenian council that met on it.
Agora
Public square of a Greek city, centre of trade, politics and conversation; in Athens, at the foot of the Acropolis.
Stoa
Colonnaded portico or hall; in Athens the Stoa of Attalos, the Stoa Poikile and the Stoa Basileios stand out.
Epicureans
Followers of Epicurus, who sought a tranquil life and taught that the gods do not intervene in human affairs.
Stoics
School founded by Zeno in the Stoa Poikile; they saw the cosmos as ordered by a divine reason, the logos.
