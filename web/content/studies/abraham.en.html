Abraham: from Ur to Canaan

Genesis tells how Terah’s family leaves Ur of the Chaldees, stops in Haran, and how Abram, called by God, reaches Canaan. We will first observe the sacred text and then the city of Ur, the debate about its location and the semi-nomadic life of the patriarchs.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what the archaeology of Mesopotamia and Canaan contributes and what remains a proposed location or date.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Ancient context helps when three things are kept apart: the cities and landscapes that can be studied, the proposed locations and dates, and the call narrated by Scripture.

Widely recognized context

Ur, a great Sumerian city

The ruins of Ur lie at Tell el-Muqayyar in southern Iraq. Leonard Woolley excavated them from the 1920s for the British Museum and the University of Pennsylvania Museum; its ziggurat was built by King Ur-Nammu around 2100 BC for the moon god Nanna.

Widely recognized context

Haran and the moon cult

Haran lay in upper Mesopotamia, between the Tigris and the Euphrates. Like Ur, it was a centre of the cult of the moon god, called Sin in Akkadian; Joshua 24:2 recalls that Israel’s ancestors “served other gods”.

Unproven identification

Which Ur?

The traditional identification with Ur in southern Babylonia is still the most widely defended, but some scholars place it in the north, near Haran and Urfa. Moreover, the Chaldeans appear in Assyrian sources of the ninth century BC, long after the patriarchal period; many explain this as a clarification for later readers. The question is not settled.

Unproven identification

The date of the patriarchs

Many authors place Abraham in the first half of the second millennium BC (Middle Bronze Age): some around 2000 BC, others around 1800 BC, while others think the accounts reflect later periods. No find allows Abraham to be dated directly.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its context

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: call, promise and faith

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that the story begins with God’s word: he calls, promises offspring, a land and a blessing for all the families of the earth, and Abram responds with obedience and faith (Genesis 12:1–4; 15:6).

Which details it helps explain

Sarai is barren (11:30), and yet the promise speaks of “a great nation”; in 15:5–6 Abram looks at the stars and believes the Lord. Hebrews 11:8 sums up his departure: he obeyed, “not knowing where he went”; Galatians 3:8 sees in 12:3 the gospel announced in advance to the nations.

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the location of Ur, the route of the journey or the date of Abraham.

02

Ur of the Chaldees and Haran

What it contributes

It presents the city where the account begins. The classic identification is Sumerian Ur at Tell el-Muqayyar, with Ur-Nammu’s ziggurat dedicated to the moon god; Haran, in upper Mesopotamia, shared that cult. An alternative proposal, defended by Cyrus Gordon, looks for a northern “Ur” near Haran and Urfa.

Which details it helps explain

Woolley’s excavations at Ur; the Hebrew name Ur Kasdim; the mention of the Chaldeans in ninth-century BC Assyrian annals; the existence of other places called Ur in cuneiform texts; and Alan Millard’s arguments for the southern city.

Which limit must remain

Archaeology describes Sumerian Ur very well, but it has found no trace of Abraham there and does not settle with certainty which Ur the account means.

03

Semi-nomadic life and the road to Canaan

What it contributes

It helps us picture Abram as a shepherd who lives in tents and moves with his flocks through the Fertile Crescent, from the upper Euphrates to the hills of Canaan, where he stops at Shechem and near Bethel.

Which details it helps explain

Shechem is identified with Tell Balata, whose first urbanization belongs to the Middle Bronze Age; the text speaks of “the place of Shechem” and an oak, not a city. The Mari archives (eighteenth century BC) show mobile pastoral groups in the region. The date of the patriarchs is debated: around 2000 BC, around 1800 BC or, for others, a later period.

Which limit must remain

Cultural parallels make the picture in the account plausible, but they do not trace the exact route or date Abraham: patriarchal chronology remains under debate.

What you can now recognize

What context contributes to the Abraham account

Sumerian Ur with its ziggurat, Haran with its moon cult and the hills of Canaan travelled by shepherds help us picture the world Abram leaves and the road he takes. But they do not allow us to state with certainty which Ur was his, to trace his route or to fix his date.

The centre of the account lies in what God says and promises, and in the faith of someone who sets out not knowing where he is going. Context illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

What does this add to my reading?

1. Look at what he leaves

According to Genesis 12:1, what does God ask Abram to leave, and what does he know of his destination?

He is to leave his country, his relatives and his father’s house, and go to a land God will show him. The destination is not named beforehand: that is why Hebrews 11:8 says he went out “not knowing where he went”. Trust in the one who calls matters more than the map.

2. What does Abram believe?

In Genesis 15:1–6, what objection does Abram raise and how does he respond in the end?

Return to Genesis 15:1–6
See a guided answer

Abram points out that he has no children and that his heir will be a servant of his household. God has him look at the stars and promises offspring as numerous as they are. Abram believes the Lord, and it is counted to him as righteousness (15:6): faith rests on God’s word before seeing its fulfilment.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Ur of the Chaldees
In Hebrew Ur Kasdim, the city Terah’s family leaves (Genesis 11:31); usually identified with Sumerian Ur at Tell el-Muqayyar.
Ziggurat
Stepped mudbrick tower crowned by a temple; the one at Ur was built by Ur-Nammu around 2100 BC for the moon god Nanna.
Haran
City in upper Mesopotamia where Terah’s family settled and from which Abram set out for Canaan.
Chaldeans
People of southern Mesopotamia first mentioned in Assyrian sources of the ninth century BC.
Middle Bronze Age
Archaeological period of Syria and Canaan in the first half of the second millennium BC, in which many place the patriarchs.
