Paul’s journeys: routes, cities and letters

Acts 13 tells how the church in Antioch sends Barnabas and Saul; from there the gospel reaches Cyprus, Asia Minor, Greece and, finally, Rome. We will first observe the sacred text and then the routes, the cities and the letters born of those journeys.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what history and archaeology contribute about the cities and dates, and what remains a reconstruction.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Paul’s journeys move through a well-documented Roman world; even so, known cities, probable dates and modern reconstructions should be kept apart.

Widely recognized context

A fixed date: Gallio in Corinth

A letter of Emperor Claudius inscribed at Delphi, from AD 52, shows that Gallio was proconsul of Achaia around 51–52. This places Paul’s stay in Corinth (Acts 18:12) around those years.

Widely recognized context

Cities that can be visited today

Philippi was a Roman colony on the Via Egnatia (Acts 16:12). At Ephesus the great theatre, which held about 25,000 people, and remains of the Temple of Artemis survive (Acts 19:27–29).

Unproven identification

The “three journeys”

Acts does not number the journeys: dividing them into three (13–14; 15:36–18:22; 18:23–21:17) is a modern way of ordering the account, and the boundary between the second and third is conventional.

Unproven identification

Exact dates and where some letters were written

Apart from the fixed point of Gallio, the dates of Paul’s life are approximate. There is no agreement on whether the letters written from prison came from Rome, Ephesus or Caesarea.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its world

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: the gospel to the ends of the earth

What it contributes

It presents the journeys as the fulfilment of Acts 1:8: the Spirit sends, God opens doors and the gospel advances from Jerusalem to Rome despite opposition.

Which details it helps explain

The sending in prayer (13:1–3), the turn to the Gentiles (13:46–47), the hardships of the road (2 Corinthians 11:25–27) and the open ending in Rome (Acts 28:31).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the dates or the exact course of each route.

02

Routes through the Roman world

What it contributes

It places the journeys on roads such as the Via Egnatia and on Mediterranean sea routes, with their distances, seasons and dangers.

Which details it helps explain

Why Philippi, a Roman colony on the Via Egnatia, is the gateway to Europe (Acts 16:12), and why the voyage to Rome depends on ships and sailing seasons (Acts 27).

Which limit must remain

Maps of the journeys are reconstructions: on several stretches Acts does not say which exact road was taken.

03

Cities and letters

What it contributes

It links the cities in Acts with the letters Paul later wrote to his churches: Philippians, Thessalonians, Corinthians, Ephesians.

Which details it helps explain

The Gallio inscription as a fixed point in the chronology, the theatre at Ephesus where the crowd gathered (Acts 19:29) and the trade around Artemis that explains the riot.

Which limit must remain

A well-excavated city confirms the setting, not every detail of the visit or the exact place where each letter was written.

What you can now recognize

What the Roman world contributes to Paul’s journeys

The roads, ports and cities such as Philippi, Corinth and Ephesus are well documented, and the Gallio inscription offers a fixed date that is rare in ancient history. This gives historical solidity to the account in Acts. But maps of “three journeys” and many dates remain reconstructions.

The centre of the account lies in God’s work: the Spirit sends, the gospel crosses boundaries and churches are born in very different cities. The Roman world illuminates the road; the sacred text defines the message.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Antioch
City in Syria where the disciples were first called Christians (Acts 11:26) and from which the journeys set out.
Via Egnatia
Roman road across Macedonia linking the Adriatic with Byzantium; Philippi and Thessalonica lay on it.
Proconsul
Roman governor of a province administered by the Senate, such as Achaia, whose capital was Corinth.
Roman colony
City with Roman status, such as Philippi (Acts 16:12), settled partly by veterans and Roman citizens.
Gentiles
People who were not Jewish; Paul’s preaching is increasingly directed to them.
