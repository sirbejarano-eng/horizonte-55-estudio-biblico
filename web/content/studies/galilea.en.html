Galilee: Capernaum and the sea

Mark 1 tells of the beginning of Jesus’ ministry in Galilee: he proclaims the kingdom, calls some fishermen and spends a Sabbath in Capernaum, between the synagogue and a house. We will first observe the sacred text and then the town, the lake and its economy as archaeology has studied them.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what archaeology has found in Capernaum and along the lake shore, and what remains a likely but unproven identification.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Capernaum and the lake shore have been thoroughly excavated, but the known town, the proposed buildings and the scene narrated by the Gospel should be kept apart.

Widely recognized context

Capernaum, a town by the lake

Capernaum lay on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee. Excavations began in 1905 under Kohl and Watzinger and were completed by the Franciscans; their work uncovered the great synagogue and the quarter of basalt houses.

Widely recognized context

Magdala: synagogue and salted fish

In 2009 a first-century synagogue came to light at Magdala, with the Magdala Stone, decorated with a menorah. The town, also called Taricheae (“salted fish”), prospered through its harbour and the export of salted fish.

Unproven identification

The house of Peter

Beneath a fifth-century octagonal church lies a house from the first century BC whose main room was plastered and used as a meeting place. The archaeological sequence is compatible with the tradition, but there is no definitive proof that it was Peter’s house.

Unproven identification

The synagogue of Mark 1:21

The white synagogue visible today is Byzantine: Corbo and Loffreda dated it to the fourth century, and others consider it somewhat earlier. Beneath it are basalt walls that many assign to the first century, but it cannot be established that they belong to the synagogue where Jesus taught.

Unproven identification

The Ginosar boat

The boat found in 1986 beside Kibbutz Ginosar is radiocarbon-dated to between 100 BC and AD 100 and is displayed at the Yigal Allon museum. It shows what boats of the time were like; the nickname “Jesus boat” is popular: nothing links it with him or his disciples.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its shore

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: the kingdom draws near in Galilee

What it contributes

It shows that God’s kingdom becomes present in Jesus’ words and works, and that he himself calls ordinary people to follow him. Matthew sees his arrival in Capernaum as the fulfilment of Isaiah: in “Galilee of the Gentiles” the people who walked in darkness see a great light (Matthew 4:12–16; Isaiah 9:1–2).

Which details it helps explain

The call to repentance and faith (Mark 1:15), the fishermen’s immediate response (1:18, 20), the authority of his teaching (1:22, 27) and the prayer that comes before the mission to other towns (1:35–38).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself identify which buildings in Capernaum are the synagogue or the house of the account.

Related readings: source 1 and source 2.

02

Capernaum: the synagogue and the house

What it contributes

It helps us picture the town where Jesus made his base: basalt houses around courtyards, a synagogue and, close by, a house that Christians venerated from very early on.

Which details it helps explain

The first-century BC house plastered after Jesus’ death, the house church of the following centuries and the fifth-century octagonal church; the white limestone synagogue, dated by Corbo and Loffreda to the fourth century, built over older basalt walls.

Which limit must remain

An ancient tradition and a well-studied sequence of buildings are not the same as a proven identification.

03

The Sea of Galilee: boats, fishing and Magdala

What it contributes

It places the calling within a real economy: families with boats and hired workers, nets cast and mended, and towns such as Magdala that lived on salted fish.

Which details it helps explain

The Ginosar boat (first century BC–first century AD), the harbour and fishing installations of Magdala, its first-century synagogue and the Magdala Stone, with the menorah and other temple symbols.

Which limit must remain

These finds describe the world of the fishermen in the account, but none belongs to a specific person or scene in the Gospel.

What you can now recognize

What Galilee contributes to the beginning of the Gospel

Capernaum was a town of basalt houses on the shore of a lake where people lived from fishing; it had synagogues, boats like the one from Ginosar and neighbouring towns such as Magdala that exported salted fish. This gives a concrete setting to the beginning of Jesus’ ministry. But it does not allow us to state with certainty which was Peter’s house or which was the synagogue of that first Sabbath.

The centre of the account lies in who Jesus is and what he does: he announces that the kingdom has drawn near, calls fishermen to follow him, teaches with authority, heals and prays before going on his way. The shore illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

What does this add to my reading?

1. Look at what they leave

According to Mark 1:16–20, what were the four fishermen doing, and what do they leave to follow Jesus?

See a guided answer

Simon and Andrew are casting the net; James and John are mending theirs in the boat. The first two leave their nets, and the other two also leave their father with the hired servants. The detail points to a family business with employees: following Jesus means leaving a real livelihood.

2. Who is this?

In Mark 4:35–41, what happens in the boat during the storm, and what do the disciples ask at the end?

Read Mark 4:35–41
See a guided answer

A great storm fills the boat with water while Jesus sleeps; he rebukes the wind and the sea, and there is a great calm. The disciples, filled with fear, ask: “Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?” The sea of their trade becomes the place where they discover who Jesus is.

Result of the journey: you can now recognize the central message of the beginning of the ministry in Galilee, place it prudently in first-century Capernaum and its lake, and distinguish proven finds from identifications that are only likely.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Capernaum
From the Hebrew Kefar Nahum, “village of Nahum”; a town on the northwestern shore of the lake where Jesus lived in Galilee (Matthew 4:13).
Gennesaret
Another name for the Sea of Galilee, also called the Lake of Tiberias and, in the Old Testament, the Sea of Chinnereth.
Synagogue
Meeting place of the Jewish community for reading and explaining the Scriptures, especially on the Sabbath.
Octagonal church
A type of Byzantine church with an eight-sided plan used to commemorate venerated places, such as the house at Capernaum.
Taricheae
Greek name of Magdala linked to salted fish, the main product the town exported.
