Solomon’s temple: a house for the name of God

Solomon builds the house of the Lord in Jerusalem, brings the ark into it and the cloud fills the temple, as it once filled the tabernacle. We will first observe the sacred text and then the temples of the region, what is and is not known about the Temple Mount, and the figure of 480 years.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what the temples excavated in the region contribute and what remains a proposal or debate about place and date.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

The Hebrew text reads 480 years in 6:1, but most Greek manuscripts of the Septuagint read 440. The measurements are given in cubits: some versions convert them to metres (about 27 long), and the “name” formula of 8:29 is translated in different ways.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Ancient context helps when three things are kept apart: what the text describes, the temples of the region that can be excavated and what is, or is not, known today about the place where the temple stood.

Widely recognized context

Temples with a tripartite plan in the region

At ’Ain Dara (northern Syria) and Tell Tayinat (southern Turkey), temples with a porch, main hall and inner sanctuary have been excavated, belonging to the same architectural tradition as the temple described in 1 Kings 6.

Widely recognized context

Shrines outside Jerusalem

In the Judahite fortress of Arad a shrine was excavated with a courtyard, altar, broad room and raised niche. Scripture itself speaks of places of worship outside Jerusalem and of reforms that removed them (2 Kings 18:4, 22).

Widely recognized context

No direct remains of the temple

No direct archaeological remains of Solomon’s temple have been found. No archaeological excavations are carried out on the Temple Mount platform, and the building was destroyed by the Babylonians in 586 BC.

Unproven identification

The exact spot on the platform

That the temple stood on the hill now occupied by the platform is the common view, but its exact position is debated: over the rock of the Dome of the Rock, further north or according to other calculations.

Unproven identification

The 480 years of 1 Kings 6:1

Some read the figure literally and use it to date the exodus; others understand it as twelve generations of 40 years. The Greek variant of 440 years raises a further question.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its context

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: the God whom heaven cannot contain puts his name there

What it contributes

It brings a paradox to the fore: Solomon builds a house for God and the cloud fills it (1 Kings 8:10–11), yet he himself confesses that not even the heaven of heavens can contain him (8:27). The temple does not enclose God; it is the place where he has put his name and toward which his people can pray, knowing that he hears from heaven and forgives (8:29–30).

Which details it helps explain

The continuity with the tabernacle: the Tent of Meeting is brought up to the temple with the ark (8:4) and the cloud fills the house as it filled the tent (Exodus 40:34–35); the conditional promise of 6:11–13, which places obedience above the building; and the echo in the New Testament: Stephen quotes Solomon’s prayer to say that the Most High does not dwell in houses made with hands (Acts 7:47–49), and Jesus speaks of the temple of his body (John 2:19–21).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the exact appearance of the building, the precise place where it stood or the date of the exodus.

02

Neighbouring temples: what they show and what they do not

What it contributes

It shows that an elongated temple with a porch, main hall and inner sanctuary fits the architecture of Syria and the northern Levant. The temple at ’Ain Dara, in use from about 1300 to 740 BC, shares more than 30 features with the Jerusalem temple, according to archaeologist John Monson; the Tell Tayinat temple, excavated by the University of Toronto, is for others the closest parallel.

Which details it helps explain

At ’Ain Dara: an artificial platform, multi-storey side chambers, lions, cherubim, sphinxes and floral motifs, and two huge footprints carved into the threshold. At Tayinat: a staircase, a porticoed entrance on a large basalt column base (compare the pillars of 1 Kings 7:21) and an inner sanctuary. At Arad, further south: a shrine of a different plan, with an altar in the courtyard, a broad room and a niche with incense altars and standing stones.

Which limit must remain

These temples show that the kind of building described in 1 Kings 6 was possible and known in the region, but they are not Solomon’s temple and do not prove every detail of the text. The dates of Tayinat and Arad are debated.

03

The Temple Mount today and the chronology

What it contributes

It explains why there is no excavation: the platform is a sacred site in use under the administration of the Islamic Waqf, and digging there is impossible. The closest thing is the Temple Mount Sifting Project, which since 2004 has been sifting soil removed from the platform in the late 1990s and has recovered objects from the Bronze Age onwards, including the First Temple period.

Which details it helps explain

The soil was removed during Waqf works in the area of the so-called Solomon’s Stables and dumped in the Kidron Valley; the project, begun by Gabriel Barkay and Zachi Dvira, has had some 200,000 volunteers. On the location, Asher Kaufman places the temple in the northwest of the inner platform; Leen Ritmeyer puts the Most Holy Place over the rock of the Dome of the Rock; David Jacobson starts from the shape of Herod’s platform. The Bible places it on Mount Moriah, at the threshing floor of Ornan (2 Chronicles 3:1). As for the date, construction is usually placed in the 960s BC; read literally, the 480 years take the exodus to the mid-fifteenth century BC.

Which limit must remain

The sifted objects lack stratigraphic context and cannot be attributed to the temple building. No location proposal has been proven, and the figure of 480 years allows both literal and schematic readings.

What you can now recognize

What context contributes to the account of Solomon’s temple

The temples of ’Ain Dara and Tell Tayinat and the Arad shrine help us picture a building with a porch, main hall and inner sanctuary in the world of the tenth century BC. But no direct remains of Solomon’s temple have been found, there is no excavation on the platform, its exact position is debated and the figure of 480 years allows more than one reading.

The centre of the account lies in what God does and promises: he dwells among his people if they walk in his ways, fills the house with his glory and hears from heaven those who pray toward the place of his name. Context illuminates the building; the sacred text defines the message.

1. Look at the condition

The text ties it to obedience: if Solomon walks in God’s commandments, God will keep his promise, dwell among Israel and not forsake them. God’s presence is not guaranteed by the building but by faithfulness to the covenant.

2. Can God fit in a house?

In 1 Kings 8:27–30, how does Solomon hold together the greatness of God and the house he has just built?

Return to 1 Kings 8:27–30
See a guided answer

He confesses that not even the heaven of heavens can contain God, much less that house. Even so, he asks that God’s eyes be open toward the place where he has put his name and that, hearing from heaven, he forgive: the temple is a place of meeting and prayer, not a dwelling that encloses God.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Ulam
The porch or vestibule at the entrance of the temple (1 Kings 6:3).
Hekal
The main hall of the temple, also called the “Holy Place”.
Debir
The inner sanctuary or Most Holy Place, where the ark was placed under the cherubim (1 Kings 6:19; 8:6).
Tripartite plan
A temple layout with three spaces in a row: porch, main hall and inner sanctuary.
Septuagint
Ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures; in 1 Kings 6:1 it reads 440 years where the Hebrew text reads 480.
