Cyrus and the return: Ezra and Nehemiah

The books of Ezra and Nehemiah tell how God moves Cyrus, king of Persia, to allow the exiles to return and how, years later, Nehemiah rebuilds the wall of Jerusalem. We will first observe the sacred text and then the Persian Empire, the Cyrus Cylinder, the province of Yehud and the debate about the Jerusalem of that time.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what the documents and finds of the Persian period show and what remains an open question of dates and archaeology.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

History and archaeology help when three things are kept apart: the documents and remains that can be studied, the specialists’ interpretations and the events narrated by Scripture.

Widely recognized context

The Cyrus Cylinder

After conquering Babylon in 539 BC, Cyrus had a clay cylinder inscribed that presents his victory as the work of Marduk and states that he restored temples and returned deported gods and peoples to their lands. It does not mention Judah or Jerusalem, and it is not the decree recorded in Ezra.

Widely recognized context

Yehud, a Persian province

Judah was a small province of the Persian Empire called Yehud. Silver coins with the Aramaic inscription “YHD” are known, and a letter from the Jews of Elephantine (408 BC) is addressed to the governor of Judah and mentions the sons of Sanballat, governor of Samaria, and the high priest Johanan.

Unproven identification

In what order did Ezra and Nehemiah arrive?

The text places Ezra in the seventh year of Artaxerxes and Nehemiah in the twentieth. If this is Artaxerxes I, Ezra arrived in 458 BC, before Nehemiah (445 BC); many specialists propose Artaxerxes II (397 BC), and others assume a copying error. The question remains open.

Unproven identification

Persian Jerusalem and its wall

Eilat Mazar proposed that a tower and a stretch of wall in the City of David were Nehemiah’s work; others date them to the Hasmonean period. Israel Finkelstein sees Persian Jerusalem as a small unwalled village; other archaeologists consider it a larger centre. It is unresolved.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and the Persian period

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: God moves a king’s heart

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that the return springs from God’s initiative: the Lord stirs up the spirit of Cyrus (Ezra 1:1) and also that of those who go up (1:5). Isaiah calls Cyrus “my shepherd” and “his anointed” (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1), and Nehemiah attributes the king’s favour to “the good hand of my God” (Nehemiah 2:8).

Which details it helps explain

The decree repeated at the end of 2 Chronicles 36:22–23, the vessels returned to the temple, the altar set up before the foundations (Ezra 3:2–3), the weeping and joy when the foundations were laid (3:10–13), Nehemiah’s prayer before the king (Nehemiah 2:4) and the Law read in public and explained to the people (Nehemiah 8:1–10).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the dates of Ezra and Nehemiah or the archaeological appearance of Persian Jerusalem.

02

Cyrus and the Persian Empire

What it contributes

It places the account in the Achaemenid Empire, which from 539 BC ruled Babylon and its provinces. The Cyrus Cylinder shows a policy of restoring temples and deported peoples that helps us understand the context of the decree in Ezra 1.

Which details it helps explain

A clay cylinder of some forty-five lines in Akkadian, found at Babylon in 1879 and kept in the British Museum; fragments of another copy identified in 2009–2010; and Persepolis, founded by Darius I in 518 BC, whose reliefs show dignitaries and tribute bearers from the peoples of the empire.

Which limit must remain

The cylinder speaks of Babylon and Marduk, not of Judah: it shows a general policy and does not confirm the specific wording of the decree in Ezra.

Related readings: source 1 and source 2 and source 3.

03

Yehud and Nehemiah’s wall

What it contributes

It explains what the province to which the exiles returned was like and why specialists debate the wall. Nehemiah, the king’s cupbearer, was governor of Judah from 445 BC, and the text says the wall was finished in 52 days (Nehemiah 6:15).

Which details it helps explain

The small silver coins with the inscription “YHD”; the Elephantine letter to the governor of Judah, which names the sons of Sanballat and the high priest Johanan (compare Nehemiah 12:22); the tower and wall in the City of David that Eilat Mazar attributed to Nehemiah because of the Persian pottery found beneath them; and the objection that such pottery only gives an earliest possible date, as well as the position of Israel Finkelstein, who sees Persian Jerusalem as a village of a few hundred inhabitants.

Which limit must remain

No stretch of the Persian wall has been securely identified: the remains are scarce, hard to date and interpreted in different ways.

What you can now recognize

What history and archaeology contribute to the return

The Persian Empire, the Cyrus Cylinder, the Yehud coins and the Elephantine papyri show a real world in which a foreign king allowed temples to be restored, Judah was a small province and figures such as Sanballat and Johanan left traces outside the Bible. But the order of Ezra and Nehemiah, the course of the wall and the size of Persian Jerusalem are still debated, so these finds should not be presented either as proof or as a refutation of the account.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he keeps his word, moves the heart of a pagan king and that of his people, and gathers Israel again around the altar, the temple and his Law. History illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

Return to the sacred text

What does this add to my reading?

1. The stirred spirit

According to Ezra 1:1–5, whom does the Lord stir up, and for what purpose?

See a guided answer

The Lord stirs up the spirit of Cyrus so that the word spoken by Jeremiah may be fulfilled and his house built in Jerusalem (1:1–2). He then stirs up the spirit of those who go up as well (1:5). The text thus presents the return as God’s work in the king and in his people, in harmony with Isaiah 44:28 and 2 Chronicles 36:22–23.

2. The Law read in public

In Nehemiah 8:1–10, what happens when Ezra reads the Law, and what does Nehemiah tell the people?

Return to Nehemiah 8:10
See a guided answer

Ezra reads from early morning until midday, the Levites explain the sense so that the people understand (8:3, 8), and the people weep as they hear the Law. Nehemiah, Ezra and the Levites tell them that the day is holy and that they should not grieve, “for the joy of the Lord is your strength” (8:10). The rebuilding does not end with the walls: it culminates in a people listening to God’s word.

Result of the journey: you can now recognize the central message of the return, place it prudently in the Persian Empire and the province of Yehud, and explain fairly why the order of Ezra and Nehemiah and the wall are still debated.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Yehud
Aramaic name of the Persian province of Judah; it appears abbreviated as “YHD” on silver coins.
Cyrus Cylinder
Clay cylinder inscribed in Akkadian after the conquest of Babylon in 539 BC; it is kept in the British Museum.
Achaemenid
Name of the Persian dynasty of Cyrus, Darius and Artaxerxes.
Elephantine
Island in the Nile in southern Egypt, home to a Jewish colony whose fifth-century BC Aramaic papyri have survived.
Cupbearer
Trusted official who served the king his wine; this was Nehemiah’s post (Nehemiah 1:11).
