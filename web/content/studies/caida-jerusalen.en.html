The fall of Jerusalem and the exile

2 Kings 25 tells of the siege of Jerusalem, the burning of the temple and the deportation of the people to Babylon; Jeremiah 39 and 52 recount the same events and Lamentations 1 mourns for the city. We will first observe the sacred text and then Jerusalem’s destruction layer, the Lachish letters and the Babylonian documents about the exiles.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what the excavations and the tablets from Babylon show, and what remains an open question of dates and numbers.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

The account dates the events by regnal years (Zedekiah’s ninth and eleventh, Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth), not by our calendar, and gives no numbers of deportees here. Jeremiah 52:12 places the burning on the tenth day of the fifth month; 2 Kings 25:8, on the seventh.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

History and archaeology help when three things are kept apart: the ruins and documents that can be studied, the specialists’ interpretations and the meaning Scripture gives to these events.

Widely recognized context

A destruction by fire

Excavations in the City of David documented buildings destroyed in the Babylonian conquest, with burn layers, Judahite iron arrowheads and bronze “Scythian” arrowheads used by foreign armies, and a group of bullae bearing Hebrew names.

Widely recognized context

The campaign of 597 BC in a Babylonian chronicle

A tablet of the Babylonian Chronicle kept in the British Museum (BM 21946) describes Nebuchadnezzar’s first campaign against Jerusalem in 597 BC, the one 2 Kings 24:10–17 connects with the deportation of King Jehoiachin.

Widely recognized context

Judeans in Babylon

Ration lists from Babylon name the king of Judah and his five sons among those receiving food from the Babylonian king, and about 200 tablets from Al-Yahudu (“town of Judah”) record the lives of exiled Judean families.

Unproven identification

587 or 586 BC?

The Bible dates the fall to Zedekiah’s eleventh year and Nebuchadnezzar’s nineteenth. Converting this to our calendar depends on how regnal years were counted and in which month the year began; that is why reference works give 587 or 586 BC.

Unproven identification

How many were exiled?

2 Kings 24:14–16 speaks of ten thousand captives in 597 BC; Jeremiah 52:28–30 gives smaller figures for three deportations (3,023, 832 and 745: 4,600 people). They may count differently or only certain groups. How many people remained in Judah is also debated.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its context

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: judgment foretold and hope

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that the fall is no political accident: the prophets had announced it as judgment for the people’s unfaithfulness (2 Kings 24:3; Jeremiah 25:8–11), and Lamentations confesses it: “The Lord is righteous, for I have rebelled against his commandment” (Lamentations 1:18). But judgment is not the last word.

Which details it helps explain

The loss of the temple, the king and the land; the lament of Lamentations 1:1 for the city sitting “alone” like a widow; Jeremiah’s letter to the exiles, asking them to seek the welfare of the city where they live and promising them “a future and a hope” (Jeremiah 29:4–11); the confession that the Lord’s mercies “are new every morning” (Lamentations 3:21–23); and the ending of the book, with Jehoiachin released from prison and eating at the king’s table (2 Kings 25:27–30).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the exact year of the fall or the number of deportees.

Related readings: source 2.

02

The traces of the destruction of 587/586 BC

What it contributes

It places the account in excavated sites: the City of David in Jerusalem, and Lachish, one of Judah’s last fortresses (Jeremiah 34:7).

Which details it helps explain

Yigal Shiloh’s excavations (1978–1985) found burnt buildings, such as the so-called “Burnt Room,” arrowheads and some fifty bullae in a house destroyed by the Babylonians; one bears the name of Gemariah son of Shaphan, like the official in whose chamber Baruch read Jeremiah’s scroll (Jeremiah 36:10). At Lachish, ostraca written shortly before the fall came to light; in letter 4 the sender says he is watching for the signal fires of Lachish because he cannot see those of Azekah.

Which limit must remain

The ruins confirm a violent destruction, but they do not narrate every detail of the account; that Gemariah or the Lachish letters refer to exactly the people and days of the Bible is probable or debated, not certain.

Related readings: source 1 and source 3 and source 4 and source 5.

03

Babylon and the exiles

What it contributes

It shows the other side of the story: the documents of the empire that defeated Judah and the traces of the exiles in Mesopotamia.

Which details it helps explain

The Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946, British Museum) on the campaign of 597 BC; the ration tablets found in Babylon (Vorderasiatisches Museum in Berlin, on Museum Island), which mention the king of Judah and his sons; and the Al-Yahudu archive, with names containing the divine name (“Yahu”), contracts and tax payments of Judean families who worked newly cultivated land, exhibited at the Bible Lands Museum Jerusalem (2015–2016).

Which limit must remain

These texts are administrative: they record rations, taxes and contracts, not the feelings or faith of the exiles, and they do not give the total number of deportees.

What you can now recognize

What history and archaeology contribute to the fall of Jerusalem

Few pages of the Old Testament have so many outside traces: burn layers and arrowheads in Jerusalem, letters written at Lachish, a Babylonian chronicle and tablets that name King Jehoiachin and Judean families in Babylon. Even so, the exact year of the fall and the number of exiles are still debated.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he keeps his word of judgment, but he does not abandon his people in exile and opens a future for them. History illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

What does this add to my reading?

1. The city’s lament

According to Lamentations 1:1–5 and 1:18, how does the poet describe Jerusalem, and to whom does he attribute what has happened?

See a guided answer

Jerusalem sits “alone,” like a widow, and Judah has gone into exile. The poet says that the Lord has afflicted her for the multitude of her transgressions (1:5) and confesses: “The Lord is righteous, for I have rebelled against his commandment” (1:18). The lament does not deny the pain, but it lives it before God.

2. Hope in exile

In Jeremiah 29:4–11, what does God ask of the exiles, and what does he promise them?

See a guided answer

He asks them to build houses, plant gardens, raise families and seek and pray for the welfare of the city where they live (29:5–7). He promises that when seventy years are completed he will visit them and bring them back, because his plans are “for welfare and not for evil,” to give them “a future and a hope” (29:10–11).

Key terms

Brief glossary

Bulla
Small clay seal that closed a papyrus document and bore the impressed name of its owner.
Ostracon
A potsherd used as a writing surface; plural, ostraca.
Riblah
City in Syria where Nebuchadnezzar had his headquarters and where Zedekiah was judged (2 Kings 25:6).
Babylonian Chronicle
Series of cuneiform tablets summarizing, year by year, the main events of the kings of Babylon.
Al-Yahudu
“Town of Judah”: a place in Babylonia where Judean exiles lived, which gives its name to an archive of about 200 tablets.

  6. British Museum: tablilla de la Crónica babilónica (BM 21946) — museum record: a chronicle of the years 605–594 BC that describes Nebuchadnezzar’s campaign against Jerusalem in 597 BC.
  7. Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek / Vorderasiatisches Museum Berlin: “Rationsliste für Gefangene mit Erwähnung des Königs Jojachin von Juda” (VAT 16378) — record of the tablet from Babylon listing oil deliveries to prisoners at court, among them the king of Judah.
  8. Biblical Archaeology Society: “Tablets of Jewish Exiles” — the Al-Yahudu archive, about 200 tablets, and the exhibition “By the Rivers of Babylon” at the Bible Lands Museum (2015–2016).
