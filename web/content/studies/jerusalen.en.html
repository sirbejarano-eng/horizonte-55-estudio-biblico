Jerusalem in the time of Jesus: the temple and the final week

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

First-century Jerusalem is one of the best-studied ancient cities, but what has been found should be kept apart from what only tradition points to.

Widely recognized context

The temple Herod enlarged

Herod the Great began enlarging the Temple Mount around 19 BC. John 2:20 mentions “forty-six years” of building. The Romans destroyed the temple in AD 70; the retaining walls, such as the Western Wall, remain today.

Widely recognized context

Pilate and Caiaphas outside the Bible

An inscription found at Caesarea in 1961 names Pontius Pilate as “prefect of Judea”. In 1990 an ossuary inscribed “Joseph, son of Caiaphas” came to light in Jerusalem; many archaeologists attribute it to the high priest of the Gospels.

Widely recognized context

The pools of Siloam and Bethesda

In 2004 a large Second Temple-period Pool of Siloam was discovered (John 9:7). Beside the Church of St Anne, the two pools of Bethesda were excavated, with porticoes like those described in John 5:2.

Unproven identification

The praetorium and the way of the cross

Many specialists place the praetorium in Herod’s former palace, by today’s Jaffa Gate; medieval tradition placed it in the Antonia Fortress, where the Via Dolorosa begins. The exact route is not known.

Unproven identification

Golgotha and the tomb

The Church of the Holy Sepulchre, built by Constantine in the fourth century on a spot that lay outside the wall in Jesus’ time, is the strongest candidate; even so, there is no definitive archaeological proof.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its city

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: Jesus and the temple

What it contributes

It shows Jesus entering as a humble king (Matthew 21:5), cleansing the temple, healing there and announcing its destruction; his death takes place “outside the gate” (Hebrews 13:12).

Which details it helps explain

Why the temple stands at the centre of the final week and why the cross is placed outside the city.

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the topography or the exact location of each scene.

What you can now recognize

What the city contributes to the Gospel accounts

First-century Jerusalem had a monumental temple, public pools and named authorities whom archaeology has found. This gives historical weight to the setting of Jesus’ final week. But it does not allow us to point with certainty to every place or to reconstruct the exact route from the trial to the cross.

The centre of the account lies in who Jesus is and what he does: he enters as king, cleanses the temple, announces its end and gives his life. The city illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Temple Mount
The platform Herod enlarged for the temple; parts of its retaining walls survive.
Praetorium
Residence and seat of the Roman governor when he was in Jerusalem; Jesus was tried there (John 18:28).
Golgotha
Place of the crucifixion, “place of a skull”, outside the wall of its time (John 19:17).
Siloam
Pool in the south of the city where Jesus sent the man born blind to wash (John 9:7).
Ossuary
Stone box in which a person’s bones were placed after a time in the tomb.
