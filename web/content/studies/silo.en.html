Shiloh and the ark in the days of the judges

The tent of meeting stood at Shiloh during the period of the judges. There Hannah prayed, there Samuel grew up, and from there the ark went out to the battle in which it was captured. We will first observe the sacred text and then the site of Khirbet Seilun, its excavations and the debate about where the Tabernacle stood.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what the excavations at Shiloh show and what remains a proposal about the location of the sanctuary.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

The text calls the sanctuary at Shiloh both the “house” or “temple” of the Lord, with doors and a doorpost (1:9; 3:3, 15), and the “tent of meeting” (2:22). It does not say where in the town it stood or narrate what happened to Shiloh after the battle; Psalm 78:60 and Jeremiah 7:12–14 recall that.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Archaeology helps when three things are kept apart: the site that can be studied, the excavators’ interpretations and the sanctuary Scripture speaks of.

Widely recognized context

A place identified since the nineteenth century

Shiloh is commonly identified with Khirbet Seilun (Tel Shiloh), in the central hill country north of Bethel. Edward Robinson proposed the identification in 1838, and the location matches Judges 21:19.

Widely recognized context

An Iron Age I village destroyed by fire

The Danish excavations (1926–1932) and those of Israel Finkelstein (1981–1984) documented pillared buildings with large storage jars, destroyed in a fierce fire. Many date that destruction to around the middle of the eleventh century BC and link it with the Philistine war of 1 Samuel 4.

Unproven identification

Where did the Tabernacle stand?

The summit of the hill, the northern terrace or scarp and, more recently, structures uncovered by the ABR team have all been proposed. None of these proposals is proven: a tent of skins and fabric leaves hardly any trace, and the summit was heavily disturbed by later building.

Unproven identification

How to interpret the cultic finds

The altar corners of 2019, the animal bones and the cultic vessels are interpreted in different ways. ABR links them with the Tabernacle; other archaeologists ask for full publication before conclusions are drawn. When Israel arrived at Shiloh is also debated, depending on the date given to the Exodus.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and the site

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: prayer, the word and the departing glory

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that God hears the prayer of a humiliated woman, speaks again through Samuel in a time of silence and refuses to be manipulated: the ark carried into battle like a charm does not guarantee victory.

Which details it helps explain

Hannah’s prayer and vow (1 Samuel 1:10–11), the lamp that had not yet gone out (3:3), Samuel’s call (3:10) and the name Ichabod (4:21–22). Psalm 78:60 says that God “forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh”, and Jeremiah 7:12–14 holds up Shiloh as a warning to those who trusted in the temple in Jerusalem.

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not indicate where on the hill the sanctuary stood or date its destruction.

Related readings: source 1.

02

Tel Shiloh: the village of Eli’s time

What it contributes

It places the account in a concrete location: a hill in the hill country of Ephraim, surrounded by fertile valleys, which was Israel’s central sanctuary in the twelfth and eleventh centuries BC and was destroyed around 1050 BC.

Which details it helps explain

A walled Canaanite town of the Middle Bronze Age; a large deposit of cultic animal bones documented by Finkelstein; in Iron Age I, pillared buildings with collared-rim jars, destroyed by fire; and later occupation down to Roman, Byzantine and medieval times. The site was excavated by a Danish expedition (1926–1932, led by Hans Kjær, and 1963), by Israel Finkelstein (1981–1984) and, since 2017, by the Associates for Biblical Research under the direction of Scott Stripling.

Which limit must remain

The remains show a real village destroyed by fire, but they bear no written name linking them to Eli, to Samuel or to the battle of Ebenezer.

Related readings: source 1 and source 2 and source 3 and source 4.

03

The site of the Tabernacle: proposals and debate

What it contributes

It explains why there are several proposals. Since the nineteenth century Charles Wilson and others have pointed to a level terrace on the northern scarp; ABR considers the summit or the northern slope and, since 2017, has presented finds that it links with the sanctuary.

Which details it helps explain

In 2019 three corners of a horned altar came to light beside a monumental Iron Age building: one had been reused in a Roman wall and another lay in a destruction layer. There are also ceramic pomegranates, a cultic chalice, storage jars and many animal bones, with very few pig bones in the Israelite levels. The archaeologist Aren Maeir cautions that most of this has not been published in detail and that neither the ark nor the Tabernacle has been found.

Which limit must remain

These proposals are one team’s interpretations, disputed by other specialists: no location of the Tabernacle has been demonstrated.

What you can now recognize

What archaeology contributes to the Shiloh account

Khirbet Seilun was an Iron Age I village with large storerooms that ended in destruction by fire, and its identification with Shiloh is widely accepted. But neither the exact site of the Tabernacle nor the interpretation of the recent cultic finds is settled, so they should be presented as proposals, not as proven discoveries.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he hears Hannah, speaks to Samuel and will not be used as a charm. When the glory departs from Shiloh, the problem is not the place but the unfaithfulness of the people. Archaeology illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

What does this add to my reading?

1. A rare word

According to 1 Samuel 3:1–10, what was the spiritual situation at Shiloh, and how does Samuel respond once he understands who is calling him?

The word of the Lord was rare and visions were not frequent, but “the lamp of God” had not yet gone out. Guided by Eli, Samuel answers: “Speak, for your servant hears.” The account presents the call as a new beginning that depends on God’s initiative.

2. Where did the glory go?

In 1 Samuel 4:21–22, what does the name Ichabod mean, and how is it related to the loss of the ark?

Return to 1 Samuel 4:21–22
See a guided answer

Ichabod can be understood as “Where is the glory?” or “No glory”. His mother explains it: “The glory has departed from Israel,” because the ark has been taken. Psalm 78:60–61 and Jeremiah 7:12–14 read that moment as judgement: God forsook Shiloh because of his people’s unfaithfulness.

Result of the journey: you can now recognize the central message of the account, place it prudently at the site of Tel Shiloh and explain fairly why the location of the Tabernacle is still debated.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Khirbet Seilun / Tel Shiloh
Site in the central hill country, north of Bethel, commonly identified with biblical Shiloh.
Ichabod
Name of Eli’s grandson (1 Samuel 4:21); understood as “Where is the glory?” or “No glory”.
Iron Age I
Archaeological period of roughly 1200–1000 BC; broadly corresponds to the period of the judges.
Collared-rim jar
Large storage vessel typical of the hill country in Iron Age I; several complete examples were found at Shiloh.
Horns of the altar
Projections at the four corners of an altar (Exodus 27:2); three fragments of this kind were found at Shiloh in 2019.
