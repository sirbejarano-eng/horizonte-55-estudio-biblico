Joseph in Egypt: from the pit to the palace

Genesis tells how Joseph, sold by his brothers, arrives in Egypt as a slave and ends up governing the land. We will first observe the sacred text and then the Egypt that received Asiatics, the customs glimpsed in the account and the debate about its date.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what ancient Egypt contributes and what remains open, such as the identity of the pharaoh and the date.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Ancient context helps when three things are kept apart: the Egypt that can be studied, the proposed dates and the message the account puts at its centre.

Widely recognized context

Asiatics in Middle Kingdom Egypt

A painting in the tomb of Khnumhotep at Beni Hasan, from the reign of Senwosret II (nineteenth century BC), shows a group of Asiatics arriving in Egypt; a papyrus in the Brooklyn Museum lists the servants of a Theban household, 45 of them Asiatics.

Widely recognized context

Avaris and the Hyksos in the Delta

Excavations at Tell el-Dab‘a, ancient Avaris, show that immigrants from the Levant settled in the eastern Delta for centuries; the Hyksos, rulers of foreign origin, had their capital there.

Unproven identification

The pharaoh and the date

Genesis does not name the pharaoh. Some place Joseph in the Middle Kingdom, others in the Hyksos period, and others think of a later setting. No known Egyptian document mentions Joseph: the question remains open.

Unproven identification

Early or late details?

Some scholars read the price of twenty shekels, the Egyptian names and the embalming as traces of the second millennium BC; others argue that they fit later periods as well, or better. The debate is not settled.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its context

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: God meant it for good

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that through envy, injustice and neglect God keeps acting: “You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good” (Genesis 50:20; see 45:5–8).

Which details it helps explain

The repeated phrase “the Lord was with Joseph” (39:2, 21, 23), the insistence that interpretation comes from God (40:8; 41:16) and the final purpose: to preserve many lives during the famine.

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the date, the pharaoh or the exact place where Joseph lived.

Related readings: source 1.

02

Asiatics in Egypt and the eastern Delta

What it contributes

It shows that people from Canaan and Syria coming into Egypt was a familiar reality. The Beni Hasan scene portrays 37 Asiatics led by Ibsha, with coloured garments and donkeys; the Brooklyn papyrus attests Asiatic servants in Egyptian households; Avaris reveals a Levantine community in the Delta.

Which details it helps explain

Beni Hasan, in Middle Egypt, around the nineteenth century BC; the Brooklyn papyrus (around 1809–1743 BC); Tell el-Dab‘a, excavated since 1966, capital of the Hyksos (Fifteenth Dynasty). Some link Joseph’s rise with that world; others stress that the Hyksos were expelled, whereas Israel left later and in a different way.

Which limit must remain

These finds describe a world in which the account is plausible, but they do not name Joseph or his family: they illustrate, they do not prove.

03

Egyptian customs reflected in the account

What it contributes

It helps us notice the Egyptian colouring of the story: dreams taken as messages and magicians who interpret them (41:8), a high official over the king’s house with his signet ring and a gold chain (41:40–42), Egyptian names such as Potiphar, Zaphenath-Paneah and Asenath (41:45), granaries in the cities during the years of plenty (41:48–49) and the embalming of Jacob and Joseph (50:2–3, 26).

Which details it helps explain

The price of twenty shekels (37:28) is a good example of the debate: Kenneth Kitchen compares it with slave prices of the early second millennium BC; Ronald Hendel notes that it matches the value set for a male aged five to twenty in Leviticus 27:5 and is not enough to date the account.

Which limit must remain

That a detail is genuinely Egyptian does not by itself tell us in which century the story took place: several of these features lasted for centuries.

What you can now recognize

What context contributes to the Joseph account

The Egypt of the Middle Kingdom and of the Hyksos, with Asiatics in the Delta, foreign servants in households and officials of Levantine origin, helps us picture the setting of the story. But it does not allow us to identify the pharaoh, fix the date or present any find as proof of Joseph.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he is with Joseph in slavery and in prison, gives him the interpretation and turns his brothers’ evil into salvation for many. Context illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

What does this add to my reading?

1. Notice the refrain

In Genesis 39, which phrase about Joseph is repeated, and in which situations does it appear?

See a guided answer

“The Lord was with Joseph” appears when he is a slave in Potiphar’s house (39:2–3) and when he is in prison (39:21, 23). The text does not say that God frees him at once, but that he is with him in the midst of injustice.

2. Who guided the story?

In Genesis 50:20, how does Joseph sum up what his brothers did and what God did?

Return to Genesis 50:20
See a guided answer

Joseph does not deny the evil: “you meant evil against me”. But he affirms that God “meant it for good”, to save many people alive. It is the same key as in 45:5–8: providence does not excuse the guilt; it overcomes it.

Result of the journey: you can now recognize the central message of the account, place it prudently in ancient Egypt and avoid presenting a particular pharaoh, date or find as proof of Joseph.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Hyksos
From Egyptian heqau khasut, “rulers of foreign lands”: a dynasty of Levantine origin that ruled northern Egypt (Fifteenth Dynasty).
Avaris / Tell el-Dab‘a
City in the eastern Delta and capital of the Hyksos, home for centuries to a population of Levantine origin.
Beni Hasan
Necropolis in Middle Egypt; the arrival of a group of Asiatics was painted in the tomb of Khnumhotep.
Aamu
Egyptian term for people from western Asia, like those in the Beni Hasan scene.
Shekel
Unit of weight used for payment in silver; Joseph was sold for twenty (Genesis 37:28).
