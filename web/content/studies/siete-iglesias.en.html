The seven churches of Revelation

From the island of Patmos, John receives a vision of the risen Christ and the task of writing to seven churches in the Roman province of Asia. We will first observe the sacred text and then the cities, the route that links them and the local parallels that many commentators propose.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what history and archaeology contribute about the seven cities and what remains a proposed interpretation.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

First, look at the sacred text

Observe before reconstructing

Read Revelation 1:9–20 and the letter to Ephesus (2:1–7) and notice the order: where is John, what does he see, where is Christ and how does he speak to the church?

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

The seven cities existed and can be visited today; even so, what is known about them, the traditions about Patmos and the local parallels commentators read into the letters should be kept apart.

Widely recognized context

Seven real cities of Asia

The seven churches were in cities of the Roman province of Asia, in the west of present-day Turkey. Pergamon, former capital of the Attalid kingdom and later capital of the province, is today a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Widely recognized context

The earthquake of AD 17

According to Tacitus, an earthquake overwhelmed twelve cities of Asia in the year 17; Sardis suffered worst, and Tiberius promised it ten million sesterces and five years free of taxes. Philadelphia is among the cities granted tax exemption.

Widely recognized context

Patmos and its tradition

The historic centre of Patmos, with the Monastery of Saint John the Theologian and the Cave of the Apocalypse, is a UNESCO World Heritage site. According to tradition, John received and dictated the revelation there.

Unproven identification

The local parallels

The “throne of Satan” at Pergamon, Laodicea’s lukewarm water, its wool and its eye salve, or Smyrna’s “crown” are proposals by commentators. Some, such as the lukewarm water, are disputed on good grounds.

Unproven identification

The date of the book

Ancient tradition, from Irenaeus on, places the vision at the end of Domitian’s reign (around AD 95); others place it in Nero’s time, in the 60s. The debate remains open.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the letters and their world

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: Christ among his church

What it contributes

It shows the risen Christ who walks among the lampstands (2:1): he knows each community, praises it, corrects it and promises it life. These are letters to real churches with a message for all.

Which details it helps explain

The praise for Ephesus’s endurance and the rebuke for its lost first love (2:2–4), the “crown of life” for Smyrna (2:10), the open door before Philadelphia (3:8) and the promises “to him who overcomes” (2:7; 3:21).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the date of the book or explain each image from the history of the city.

Related readings: source 1.

02

The route of Asia and the island of Patmos

What it contributes

It places the letters in a network of cities linked by roads: the order of Revelation 1:11 begins at Ephesus, runs north to Pergamon and comes back south through the interior to Laodicea. Many commentators see here a circuit a messenger could travel.

Which details it helps explain

Patmos as a place of exile (1:9); Pergamon, former Attalid capital, with its acropolis and Great Altar; and the debate about the date, tied to whether or not there was a systematic persecution under Domitian.

Which limit must remain

The text does not describe the messenger’s journey: the “postal route” is a reasonable reconstruction, not a fact stated by the book.

Related readings: source 2 and source 3 and source 8.

03

Local details: proposals and debates

What it contributes

It explains why many commentators read the letters in light of each city: the Great Altar of Pergamon, now in Berlin, or the imperial cult as the “throne of Satan” (2:13); Sardis and Philadelphia, shaken by the earthquake of AD 17; Laodicea, “neither cold nor hot” (3:15–16), with its aqueduct, its wool and its eye salve (3:18).

Which details it helps explain

According to a widespread proposal, Laodicea’s water arrived lukewarm by aqueduct, unlike the hot water of Hierapolis and the cold water of Colossae. Others reply that wool and textiles were common in almost all seven cities and that there is no direct evidence of a Laodicean eye salve.

Which limit must remain

A local parallel may illuminate an image, but it is not the required key to the letter: several of these details remain under discussion.

What you can now recognize

What Asia contributes to reading the seven letters

The seven cities, the acropolis of Pergamon and the earthquake of AD 17 are well-documented realities that give historical weight to the letters. But the identification of the “throne of Satan”, Laodicea’s lukewarm water and other local parallels remain proposals, and the date of the book is still debated.

The centre of the passage is Christ, who walks among the lampstands: he knows each church, calls it to return and promises life to the one who overcomes. History illuminates the cities; the sacred text defines the message.

Return to the sacred text

What does this add to my reading?

1. Where is Christ?

According to Revelation 1:12–13 and 1:20, where does John see Christ, and what do the lampstands represent?

John sees him among the seven lampstands, and the text explains that the lampstands are the churches. Before he corrects anyone, Christ appears present among his people, not far from them.

2. At the door

In Revelation 3:20–22, what does Christ offer a church he has just rebuked for being lukewarm?

See a guided answer

He knocks at the door and promises to come in and dine with whoever opens, and to give the one who overcomes a place on his throne. Even the sternest letter ends with an invitation, and with the call to all the churches to hear what the Spirit says.

Result of the journey: you can now recognize the central message of the seven letters, place them prudently in the province of Asia and distinguish firm data, such as the earthquake of AD 17, from local parallels that are still under discussion.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Lampstand
Stand for oil lamps; in Revelation 1:20 it symbolizes each of the seven churches.
“To him who overcomes”
Formula with which each letter introduces a promise for the one who remains faithful to the end.
Province of Asia
Roman province in the west of present-day Turkey where the seven cities lay.
Imperial cult
Religious honours paid to the emperor and to Rome, with temples in cities such as Pergamon.
Eye salve
Ointment for the eyes; Christ advises Laodicea to anoint its eyes with it so that it may see (Revelation 3:18).
