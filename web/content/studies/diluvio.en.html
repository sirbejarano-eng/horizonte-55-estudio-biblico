The Flood: Noah and the Mesopotamian accounts

Genesis tells how God judges an earth filled with violence, preserves Noah and his family in the ark and then makes a covenant with the whole creation. We will first observe the sacred text and then the accounts from Mesopotamia, the excavations and the debate about Ararat.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Ancient context helps when three things are kept apart: the Mesopotamian accounts that can be read, the traces of floods that can be excavated and the event narrated by Scripture with its own message.

Widely recognized context

Mesopotamia preserved accounts of a great flood

The Epic of Atrahasis, in Old Babylonian and Assyrian versions, and Tablet XI of Gilgamesh, copied in the seventh century BC for Ashurbanipal’s library, tell of a flood with a hero who builds a vessel.

Widely recognized context

Ararat is Urartu

The biblical name Ararat corresponds to Urartu, a kingdom that flourished between the Aras River and the upper Tigris. Genesis 8:4 speaks of “the mountains” of that region, not of a single peak.

Widely recognized context

There were great floods in southern Mesopotamia

Leonard Woolley found at Ur a thick layer of silt without human remains; similar deposits were found at Shuruppak and Kish, but from different dates.

Unproven identification

A global or a regional flood?

Among believers who accept Scripture there are different readings: some understand a flood that covered the whole planet; others, one that reached all humanity across a wide region. It is a debate about interpretation, not a boundary between faith and unbelief.

Unproven identification

The place of the ark

Mount Ararat (Ağrı Dağı), Mount Cudi and the Durupınar site have been proposed as the place of the ark, but none is proven and no verified remains have been found.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its context

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: judgement, grace and covenant

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that God takes seriously the evil that corrupts his creation, yet does not abandon it: he preserves Noah, remembers him and binds himself to all life through a covenant whose sign is the bow in the cloud (Genesis 9:12–17).

Which details it helps explain

God’s grief over wickedness (6:6), the grace that comes before Noah’s obedience (6:8), the phrase “God remembered Noah” (8:1) and the promise that sustains the seasons while the earth remains (8:22).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself decide whether the flood was global or regional: both positions are defended from faith in the authority of Scripture.

02

The accounts from Mesopotamia

What it contributes

It shows that Genesis was written in a world that already told of a great flood. Atrahasis, Gilgamesh XI and the Ark Tablet share with Noah the advance warning, the vessel sealed with bitumen, the animals, the birds and the final sacrifice.

Which details it helps explain

The Old Babylonian Ark Tablet (around 1900–1700 BC) describes a round vessel and uses an expression meaning “two by two”; in Gilgamesh, Utnapishtim releases a dove, a swallow and a raven. The differences weigh as much as the similarities: there the gods are troubled by human noise and hide their plan; in Genesis the one God judges violence, warns Noah and ends with a covenant.

Which limit must remain

The similarities do not by themselves settle how the texts are related: some scholars see Genesis as dependent on Mesopotamia; others, a common memory handed down independently.

03

The ark, Ararat and the flood layers

What it contributes

It places the end of the account in Urartu, north of Mesopotamia, and explains what has been proposed and what has been excavated. The best-known tradition associates the ark with the great Mount Ararat; other traditions prefer Mount Cudi, and Gilgamesh places its boat on Mount Nisir.

Which details it helps explain

Durupınar is a boat-shaped formation near Ararat that even researchers who hold to a global flood regard as geological. Woolley found at Ur (around 1929) a layer of silt that he linked with the flood; Kramer dated it to around 3500 BC and connected the deposits at Shuruppak and Kish, around 3000 BC, with the tradition of Ziusudra.

Which limit must remain

The silt layers prove local floods of different dates, not the flood of Genesis; and no search for the ark has produced verified remains.

What you can now recognize

What context contributes to the flood account

The tablets of Mesopotamia show that the memory of a great flood was ancient and widespread, and the excavations at Ur, Kish and Shuruppak confirm that the region suffered severe floods. But neither the tablets nor the silt allow us to point to the mountain of the ark or to settle whether the flood was global or regional.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he judges violence, finds a righteous man, remembers him and opens a new beginning with a covenant for the whole earth. Context illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

The text points to the wickedness of the human heart and the violence that filled the earth, and it shows God’s grief. In Atrahasis the gods are troubled by human noise; Genesis instead presents a moral judgement by the one God, who warns Noah.

In Genesis 9:8–17, with whom does God make his covenant, and what sign does he give?

God makes the covenant with Noah, with his descendants and with every living creature, and sets his bow in the cloud as a sign. The account does not end with the water but with a promise: God himself pledges never again to destroy all flesh by a flood (9:11, 15).

Key terms

Brief glossary

Atrahasis
Babylonian epic in which the wise Atrahasis, warned by the god Enki, builds a vessel to survive the flood.
Utnapishtim
The flood hero in Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh; in Sumerian tradition he is called Ziusudra.
Ark Tablet
Old Babylonian tablet in the British Museum that describes a round vessel for the flood.
Urartu
Ancient kingdom between the Aras River and the upper Tigris; the Hebrew Ararat corresponds to this name.
Cubit
Ancient measure of length, from the elbow to the fingertips; Genesis uses it for the dimensions of the ark.
