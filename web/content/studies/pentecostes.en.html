Pentecost and the church in Jerusalem

Acts 2 tells how, at the feast of Pentecost, the Holy Spirit comes upon the disciples gathered in Jerusalem, Peter preaches to a crowd from many nations and a new community is born. We will first observe the sacred text and then the festival, the city of pilgrims and the places that tradition associates with the first church.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what history and archaeology teach about the Feast of Weeks and first-century Jerusalem, and what remains tradition or debate.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

The festival, the pilgrims and the synagogues of Jerusalem are well documented, but that known city, the places proposed by tradition and the event narrated in Acts should be kept apart.

Widely recognized context

Shavuot, a pilgrim festival

Pentecost (from the Greek for “fiftieth”) is the Feast of Weeks, Shavuot, celebrated seven weeks after Passover with the firstfruits of the wheat (Leviticus 23:15–21). It was one of the three feasts on which men were to appear before God (Deuteronomy 16:16).

Widely recognized context

The southern approaches to the temple

The excavations of Benjamin Mazar (1968–1978) and later of Ronny Reich uncovered the broad stairway before the southern gates of the Temple Mount and many public ritual baths (miqvaot) where pilgrims purified themselves.

Widely recognized context

A synagogue for those from abroad

The Theodotus inscription, found in 1913 near the Temple Mount and kept in the Israel Museum, commemorates a priest who built a synagogue for reading the Law, with lodging and baths “for those needing them from abroad”. It dates to before AD 70.

Unproven identification

The “upper room” on Mount Zion

Since the fourth century Christians have venerated the upper room and the place of Pentecost on Mount Zion, where the church of Holy Zion was built. The present Cenacle building, however, is from the Crusader period (twelfth century). It is an ancient tradition, not a proven identification.

Unproven identification

In a house or in the temple?

Acts 2:2 speaks of “the house”, and 1:13 of an upper room where the apostles were staying. Some interpreters suggest the public scene took place in the temple precincts, because of the gathered crowd and because the believers went there every day (2:46). The text does not allow a decision.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its city

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: the promised Spirit and a new people

What it contributes

It shows that God keeps his promise: the Spirit that Joel announced “on all flesh” (Joel 2:28–32) is poured out by the risen and exalted Jesus. Many Christian readers have seen here Babel reversed: there the confusion of languages scattered the peoples (Genesis 11:1–9); here each one hears in his own language “the mighty works of God” (Acts 2:11), and from that hearing a community is born.

Which details it helps explain

The promise of the Father (Acts 1:4–8), the proclamation of Jesus as “both Lord and Christ” (2:36), the call to repentance and to baptism “for the forgiveness of sins” (2:38) and the shared life: teaching, fellowship, bread and prayer (2:42–47).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not locate the house or the building where Pentecost took place, and the link with Babel is an ancient Christian reading, not an explicit statement by Luke.

02

Shavuot and the pilgrimage to Jerusalem

What it contributes

It explains why there were Jews “from every nation” in Jerusalem: Shavuot was a pilgrim festival, and the Jewish diaspora, spread from Mesopotamia to Rome (Acts 2:9–11), went up to the temple with the firstfruits of the harvest.

Which details it helps explain

The two loaves of the firstfruits (Leviticus 23:17); Josephus mentions great crowds in Jerusalem at Pentecost (Antiquities 17.254; War 2.42–43); the stairway and gates south of the Temple Mount and the ritual baths of the Ophel, where pilgrims purified themselves before entering.

Which limit must remain

These data describe the city of the festival, but they do not tell how many pilgrims came that year; the link between Shavuot and the giving of the Torah at Sinai is attested mainly in later Jewish sources.

03

The church in Jerusalem: houses, temple and synagogues

What it contributes

It helps us picture where the first believers met: in private houses, in the temple court—in “Solomon’s Porch” (Acts 3:11; 5:12)—and in a city full of synagogues that welcomed Jews arriving from abroad.

Which details it helps explain

The Theodotus inscription (first century), with its synagogue, lodging and baths for visitors from abroad; the Mount Zion tradition, attested from the fourth century, the Byzantine church of Holy Zion and the present twelfth-century Cenacle with its medieval pilgrim inscriptions.

Which limit must remain

A fourth-century tradition and a Crusader building do not prove where the upper room was; and although some have linked the Theodotus inscription with the “synagogue of the Freedmen” (Acts 6:9), that link is not proven.

What you can now recognize

What the festival city contributes to Pentecost

Pentecost was Shavuot, a pilgrim festival that filled Jerusalem with Jews from the land of Israel and from the diaspora; they climbed the southern stairway, purified themselves in ritual baths and gathered in synagogues such as that of Theodotus, intended for those who came from abroad. This gives a concrete setting to Acts 2. But it does not allow us to state where the house was, or whether the Cenacle on Mount Zion preserves that place.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he keeps his promise by pouring out the Spirit, lets people of many languages hear the gospel, proclaims Jesus as Lord and Christ and gathers a community that continues in teaching, fellowship, bread and prayer. The city illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

They continued steadfastly in the apostles’ teaching and fellowship, in the breaking of bread and prayer; they shared their goods with those in need. Every day they went to the temple together and broke bread in their homes. The first church lives between temple and home, and “the Lord added to the assembly day by day those who were being saved”.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Pentecost
From the Greek pentēkostē, “fiftieth”: the fiftieth day after Passover, the Greek name for the Feast of Weeks.
Shavuot
Hebrew for “weeks”; the festival of the firstfruits of the wheat harvest, seven weeks after Passover (Leviticus 23:15–21).
Diaspora
Jewish communities living outside the land of Israel, from Mesopotamia to Rome (Acts 2:9–11).
Mikveh
A stepped ritual bath for purification by immersion; plural miqvaot.
Cenacle
Name of the building on Mount Zion that tradition identifies with the upper room of the Last Supper and of Pentecost.
