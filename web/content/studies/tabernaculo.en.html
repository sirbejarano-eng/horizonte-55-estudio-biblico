The tabernacle in the wilderness: God dwells among his people

At Sinai, God asks Israel to make him a sanctuary so that he may dwell among them, and at the end of the book his glory fills the tent. We will first observe the sacred text and then the tent shrines of the ancient world, the furnishings, the materials and today’s models.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what ancient Near Eastern parallels contribute and what is a modern reconstruction or proposal.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Ancient context helps when three things are kept apart: what the text describes, the objects and tents of the ancient world that can be studied, and modern reconstructions.

Widely recognized context

Sacred tents in the ancient Near East

Texts from Mari (eighteenth century BC) and Ugarit (Late Bronze Age) speak of tents for gods and use terms related to the Hebrew words for “tabernacle” and for the tent’s frames.

Widely recognized context

Chests carried on poles in Egypt

Tutankhamun’s tomb (fourteenth century BC) contained a chest with poles that slide through rings, a system reminiscent of the ark’s poles (Exodus 25:13–15).

Widely recognized context

Purple in the region around 1000 BC

At Timna, textiles dyed with purple from sea snails were found and dated to around 1000 BC: a rare and costly dye like the one the text mentions.

Unproven identification

Ramesses II’s tent as a model

Some scholars propose that the tabernacle resembles Ramesses II’s camp at Kadesh, depicted in Egyptian reliefs. It is a suggestive parallel, not a proven connection.

Unproven identification

The exact form and whereabouts of the ark

Today’s models are interpretations of the text and differ in detail. The ark has not been found: its fate has been unclear since the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its context

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: God dwells among his people

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that the holy God chooses to dwell among Israel (Exodus 25:8) and that his glory fills the tent (40:34). The Most Holy Place, with the ark and the cherubim, is understood as God’s throne room on earth.

Which details it helps explain

The freewill offering (25:2), the pattern that comes from God (25:9), the cloud that guides each journey (40:36–38) and the echo in the New Testament: the Word “dwelt among us” (John 1:14; the Greek verb evokes “pitching a tent”), and Hebrews 8–9 reads the tabernacle as a copy and shadow of a greater sanctuary.

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the exact form of the tent or tell us what became of the ark.

02

The tent and its furnishings in their world

What it contributes

It shows that a portable sacred tent and a chest carried on poles fit the ancient world. Some scholars compare the tabernacle with Ramesses II’s camp at Kadesh, depicted at Abu Simbel, Luxor and the Ramesseum: a courtyard facing east and an elongated tent with a square inner room.

Which details it helps explain

The divine tents of Mari and Ugarit; Tutankhamun’s chest with sliding poles; chests, processional barques and winged figures of Egyptian ritual furniture; and measurements in cubits: about 30 cubits long by 10 wide and high for the tent (almost 14 metres long), and two and a half by one and a half cubits for the ark (25:10).

Which limit must remain

A similarity of form does not prove that the tabernacle copied a specific model, nor does it date the account. The ark has not been found, and no known object can be identified with it.

Related readings: source 2 and source 4 and source 5 and source 6.

03

Materials, crafts and reconstructions

What it contributes

It helps us picture the work: acacia wood, metals, skins, goats’ hair and linen dyed blue, purple and scarlet. The Timna textiles confirm that purple from sea snails, costly and hard to obtain, was used in the region around 1000 BC.

Which details it helps explain

The craftsmen Bezalel and Oholiab, filled with the Spirit of God to work gold, stone, wood and fabrics (Exodus 31:1–6; 35:30–35); the same dye could yield purple or blue; and the model in Timna Park, which reproduces the tent, the altar, the bronze basin, the table, the lampstand and the ark.

Which limit must remain

The Timna textiles are later than the wilderness period and do not come from the tabernacle; the models are modern reconstructions based on the text, not archaeological finds.

What you can now recognize

What context contributes to the account of the tabernacle

The sacred tents of Mari and Ugarit, Egyptian chests with poles and the purple found at Timna help us picture a portable tent made of precious materials. But they do not allow us to reconstruct every detail with certainty, to prove a specific model or to locate the ark.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he asks for a place to dwell among his people, fills the tent with his glory and guides them on every journey. Context illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

The text says it plainly: so that he may dwell among his people. The sanctuary is not an end in itself; its meaning lies in God’s presence with Israel along the way.

2. What fills the tent?

In Exodus 40:34–38, what happens when the tent is finished, and what role does the cloud play afterwards?

The cloud covers the tent and the glory of the Lord fills it, so that Moses cannot enter (40:35). Afterwards the cloud shows when the people are to set out and when to stay: the God who dwells in the tent is also the one who guides the way.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Mishkan
Hebrew word translated “tabernacle”; it means “dwelling” and is related to the verb “to dwell”.
Tent of Meeting
Another name for the tabernacle (Exodus 40:34), the place where God meets with his people.
Ark of the Covenant
Chest of acacia wood overlaid with gold that held the tablets of the Law; its fate is unclear and it has not been found.
Cubit
Unit of length based on the forearm; usually reckoned at about 45 centimetres.
Purple (argaman)
Costly dye obtained from sea snails; the same dye could also yield blue (tekhelet).
