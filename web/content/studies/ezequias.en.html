Hezekiah and the Siloam Tunnel

The second book of Kings tells how the Assyrian king Sennacherib invades Judah, how Hezekiah brings his distress to the temple and how Jerusalem is delivered. We will first observe the sacred text and then the Siloam Tunnel, other finds from Hezekiah’s Jerusalem and what the Assyrian sources themselves say.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what the tunnel, the seals and the Assyrian documents show, and what remains an open question.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Archaeology helps when three things are kept apart: the objects and works that can be studied, the specialists’ interpretations and the event narrated by Scripture.

Widely recognized context

Hezekiah in sources from his own time

Hezekiah’s name appears on an impression of his royal seal found on the Ophel in Jerusalem (“Belonging to Hezekiah, son of Ahaz, king of Judah”) and in the Assyrian annals of Sennacherib, written a few years after 701 BC.

Widely recognized context

The Assyrian campaign of 701 BC

Sennacherib’s annals and the reliefs from his palace at Nineveh, now in the British Museum, show that the Assyrian army ravaged Judah and conquered Lachish, as 2 Kings 18:13–17 says.

Widely recognized context

An Iron Age tunnel

The tunnel carries water from the Gihon Spring to the Pool of Siloam. Its inscription in Old Hebrew, found in 1880, tells how the two work crews met, and a radiometric study in 2003 placed it in Iron Age II, around 700 BC.

Unproven identification

What happened to the Assyrian army?

2 Kings 19:35 attributes the withdrawal to the action of the angel of the Lord. The Assyrian annals mention neither a disaster nor the capture of Jerusalem; some specialists suggest that trouble in Babylon contributed to the withdrawal. Exactly how it came about cannot be determined from the available sources.

Unproven identification

The date of the tunnel and of the reign

Reich and Shukron date the tunnel about a century before Hezekiah on the basis of pottery found in a fill; others keep the date of his reign. In addition, 2 Kings 18:13 points to an accession around 715 BC, while 18:1 and 18:9–10 place it before the fall of Samaria (722 BC). Both questions are still debated.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its finds

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: Hezekiah’s trust and the deliverance

What it contributes

It brings to the fore the Rabshakeh’s question: “What confidence is this in which you trust?” (2 Kings 18:19). The account answers by showing a king who trusts neither Egypt nor his walls but the Lord, and a God who defends his city “for my own sake, and for my servant David’s sake” (19:34).

Which details it helps explain

The praise in 18:5 (“He trusted in the Lord, the God of Israel”), the letter spread out in the temple and the prayer of 19:15–19, Isaiah’s word (19:6–7, 32–34) and the deliverance (19:35–37). Isaiah 36–37 repeats the account almost word for word, and 2 Chronicles 32 adds the exhortation: “With him is an arm of flesh, but with us is the Lord our God” (32:8).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the date of the waterworks or explain how the Assyrian withdrawal came about.

Related readings: source 1.

02

Hezekiah’s Jerusalem: tunnel, wall and seal

What it contributes

It places the account in concrete works and objects. The Siloam Tunnel, cut through the rock, carries water from the Gihon Spring to the Pool of Siloam, and its inscription tells how the two crews heard each other through the rock and met. In the Jewish Quarter a stretch of the “Broad Wall”, about 7 metres thick, has been preserved.

Which details it helps explain

The six-line inscription (found in 1880, now in the Istanbul Archaeological Museum) gives the tunnel a length of 1200 cubits, a little over half a kilometre. The study by Frumkin, Shimron and Rosenbaum (2003) dated the plaster and the stalactites by radiocarbon and uranium-thorium. The Broad Wall is linked with Isaiah 22:9–10 and 2 Chronicles 32:5. On the Ophel, Eilat Mazar found a bulla just over a centimetre across bearing Hezekiah’s name, a winged sun and two symbols of life.

Which limit must remain

The Siloam inscription names no king: the attribution to Hezekiah rests on 2 Kings 20:20, 2 Chronicles 32:30 and the dating, and some propose earlier dates.

03

Sennacherib’s campaign of 701 BC

What it contributes

It lets us read the same conflict from the Assyrian side. Sennacherib’s annals, preserved on clay prisms such as the Taylor Prism (British Museum), the one in the Oriental Institute in Chicago and the one in the Israel Museum, say that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem “like a bird in a cage” and received a large tribute.

Which details it helps explain

The Lachish reliefs, carved for the palace at Nineveh (around 700–692 BC), show the siege and capture of the city where Sennacherib received Hezekiah’s payment (2 Kings 18:14). As the Israel Museum notes, the prism describes neither the conquest nor an explicit siege of Jerusalem; it thus agrees with 2 Kings 19:32 that the city was not taken, although each source presents the outcome in its own way.

Which limit must remain

The Assyrian annals are royal propaganda: they highlight victories and pass over setbacks, so on their own they are not enough to reconstruct what happened before Jerusalem.

Related readings: source 1 and source 7 and source 8.

What you can now recognize

What archaeology and the Assyrian sources contribute to the Hezekiah account

Few biblical episodes have so many external witnesses: Hezekiah’s name on his own seal, the Siloam Tunnel and its inscription, a defensive wall, Sennacherib’s annals and the Lachish reliefs. Even so, the exact date of the tunnel, the chronology of the reign and the way the Assyrian army withdrew remain open questions.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he hears a cornered king who brings the threat into the temple, and he defends his city so that all kingdoms may know that he alone is God. Archaeology illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

The Rabshakeh mocks trust in Egypt, which he calls a “bruised reed”, and trust in the Lord. The question runs through the whole account: Judah has walls, water and tribute, but the text shows that its true security lies in God.

2. The prayer in the temple

In 2 Kings 19:14–19, what does Hezekiah do with Sennacherib’s letter, and what does he ask for in his prayer?

Hezekiah spreads the letter before the Lord, acknowledges him as the only God of all the kingdoms and maker of heaven and earth, and asks to be saved “that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that you, Lord, are God alone” (19:19). His request looks beyond his own safety: to the glory of God.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Gihon
Spring on the eastern slope of the City of David, the main water source of ancient Jerusalem (2 Chronicles 32:30).
Siloam inscription
Six-line text in Old Hebrew carved into the tunnel wall; it tells of the meeting of the two crews of stonecutters.
Bulla
Small lump of clay bearing a seal impression, used to close and authenticate documents.
Rabshakeh
Title of a high Assyrian official; in 2 Kings 18 he is Sennacherib’s spokesman before the walls of Jerusalem.
Sennacherib Prism
Clay prism bearing the Assyrian king’s annals in cuneiform script; several almost identical copies survive.
