Bethlehem and the birth of Jesus

Luke 2 tells how Jesus is born in Bethlehem, the city of David, and how some shepherds receive the news; Matthew 2 adds the visit of the wise men from the east. We will first observe the sacred text and then first-century Bethlehem, its peasant house, its basilica and the debates about the date and the star.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what history and archaeology know about Bethlehem and what remains a proposal, such as the exact date or the nature of the star.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

The Gospels name Bethlehem, the manger, the house and the star, but they do not describe the building, give a date in our calendar or explain what the star was.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Bethlehem is well placed in biblical geography and history, but what is known about the village and the basilica should be kept apart from the proposals about the date and the star.

Widely recognized context

Bethlehem, the city of David

Bethlehem lies about 10 km south of Jerusalem. The Bible presents it as David’s home town (1 Samuel 16:1; 17:12), and Micah 5:2 announces that from there will come the one who will rule Israel.

Widely recognized context

The Church of the Nativity

Since the second century a cave in Bethlehem has been venerated as the place of the birth. Constantine dedicated a basilica over it in 339; after a fire, Justinian rebuilt it in the sixth century. UNESCO inscribed it as a World Heritage site in 2012.

Unproven identification

The date of the birth

Matthew places the birth in the days of Herod, who died around 4 BC (some propose 1 BC); for this reason many estimate a date around 7–4 BC. No date has been proven.

Unproven identification

The census of Quirinius

Josephus places a census by Quirinius in Judea in AD 6, after Herod. Solutions have been proposed: an earlier census, an earlier post held by Quirinius, or reading Luke 2:2 as “before” that census. None is confirmed by inscriptions.

Unproven identification

The star of the wise men

A conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in 7 BC, a nova or a comet have been proposed. They are hypotheses: none has been proven, and Matthew 2:9 describes a star that leads and stops over a place.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its place

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: the Son of David is born poor

What it contributes

It shows that the promised Messiah is born in the city of David (Luke 2:11; Micah 5:2), yet laid in a manger; the first news goes to shepherds, and the first foreigners who worship him come from far away.

Which details it helps explain

Why Luke stresses David’s line and the humility of the place, and why the “good news” is for the lowly and for all the people (Luke 2:10).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the date, the specific building or the nature of the star.

Related readings: source 1.

02

The Bethlehem house and the Church of the Nativity

What it contributes

It helps us picture where Jesus was born: a peasant house with the animals on the lower level and mangers in the floor, and the cave Christian tradition has venerated since the second century.

Which details it helps explain

The katalyma as a “guest room” (Luke 2:7; 22:11) as opposed to the “inn” of Luke 10:34, the “house” of Matthew 2:11, the cave mentioned by Justin Martyr and Origen, and the basilicas of Constantine and Justinian.

Which limit must remain

Kenneth Bailey’s reading is widely accepted but not unanimous, and the grotto is an ancient tradition, not a place proven by archaeology.

Related readings: source 2 and source 3 and source 4.

03

The census, the date and the star

What it contributes

It places the birth in Roman history: the reign of Augustus, the end of Herod and the census of Quirinius, and it presents the astronomical proposals about the star.

Which details it helps explain

Herod dies around 4 BC; Josephus dates Quirinius’s census to AD 6; the solutions proposed for Luke 2:2; the conjunction of 7 BC, novae and comets.

Which limit must remain

The sources do not allow us to fix the exact year or identify the star; several proposals stand with differing strength.

What you can now recognize

What history contributes to the birth account

Bethlehem is a real village south of Jerusalem, linked with David from ancient times, and its basilica preserves a Christian memory that goes back to the second century. Roman history places the birth at the end of Herod’s reign. But it does not allow us to fix the exact year, fully resolve the census of Quirinius or identify the star.

The centre of the account lies in who the child is: the Saviour, the Christ, the Lord, born in the city of David and laid in a manger. History illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message: great joy for all the people.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Katalyma
Greek word in Luke 2:7, translated “inn”; in Luke 22:11 it refers to a guest room.
Manger
Feeding trough for animals; in peasant houses it could be cut into the floor or made of stone.
Wise men (magi)
Sages from the east, probably observers of the sky; Matthew does not say there were three or that they were kings.
Census
Registration of people and property ordered by Rome, mainly for tax purposes.
Grotto of the Nativity
Cave beneath the Church of the Nativity venerated by tradition as the place of the birth.
