The City of David: Zion and the king’s hill

2 Samuel 5 tells how David, now king over all Israel, takes the Jebusite stronghold of Zion and makes it the City of David. We will first observe the sacred text and then Jerusalem’s southeastern hill, the references to David outside the Bible and the debate about what his kingdom was like.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what the excavations and inscriptions show and what remains an interpretation under debate.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

The account is very brief: it does not describe the size of the city or the line of its walls, and verse 8 is one of the most difficult in the book; the Hebrew word rendered “water shaft” (tsinnor) is rare and its exact meaning is debated.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Archaeology helps when three things are kept apart: the hill and the finds that can be studied, the excavators’ interpretations and the event narrated by Scripture.

Widely recognized context

The hill beside the spring

The City of David is the narrow southeastern hill of Jerusalem, the oldest part of the city, beside the Gihon Spring, which supplied it with water all year round.

Widely recognized context

The “House of David” outside the Bible

The Aramaic stele from Tel Dan, from the ninth century BC (around 842–806 BC), found in 1993–1994, mentions a “king of Israel” and a “king of the House of David”. Most specialists understand it as a reference to David’s dynasty, although some have proposed other readings.

Unproven identification

Which way did they get in?

For years it was thought that Joab climbed up Warren’s Shaft. Ronny Reich and Eli Shukron concluded that the tunnel leading to that shaft reached it only by accident in the eighth century BC, nearly 300 years after David. How the stronghold was taken remains unresolved.

Unproven identification

David’s palace?

In 2005 Eilat Mazar proposed that the “Large Stone Structure” at the top of the hill was David’s tenth-century BC palace; Israel Finkelstein and others assign those walls to several later phases. There is no consensus.

Unproven identification

What was the kingdom like in the tenth century BC?

Finkelstein’s “low chronology” sees David’s Jerusalem as a modest village; Mazar, Garfinkel and others see signs of a central authority, such as the fortified town of Khirbet Qeiyafa, dated by carbon-14 to the late eleventh and early tenth centuries BC. The debate remains open.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and the hill

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: Zion, the chosen city

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that David’s success is not explained by his strategy but because “the Lord God of Armies was with him” (2 Samuel 5:10). The Jebusite stronghold becomes the king’s city and, soon after, the place God chooses to dwell among his people.

Which details it helps explain

The ark arrives in the City of David with shouts of joy (2 Samuel 6:12–17), and God promises David a house and a lasting kingdom (7:11–16). The Psalms sing of Zion as the mountain the Lord has chosen (Psalm 132:13; 78:68–69), and the name comes to stand for all Jerusalem and its people.

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the extent of the city, the site of the palace or the archaeological date of David’s reign.

02

The southeastern hill: water, walls and a disputed palace

What it contributes

It places the account in a concrete location: the narrow ridge south of today’s Temple Mount, between deep valleys, beside the Gihon Spring. It explains why so small a stronghold could seem impregnable and why access to water was decisive.

Which details it helps explain

Warren’s Shaft and the spring tunnels, which according to Reich and Shukron were not in use in David’s time, and the great towers that protected the water. At the top, the Stepped Stone Structure and the “Large Stone Structure” excavated by Eilat Mazar from 2005, which she dated to the tenth century BC on the basis of the pottery, Hiram of Tyre (5:11) and the statement that David “went down” to the stronghold (5:17). Finkelstein, Singer-Avitz, Herzog and Ussishkin replied that the walls belong to different phases, partly from the ninth century BC and partly much later (the Hellenistic period).

Which limit must remain

Neither Joab’s route nor David’s palace has been securely identified: both depend on how the text, the layers and the pottery are read.

03

David outside the Bible and the chronology debate

What it contributes

It explains what is known more firmly and what is debated. The Tel Dan Stele, probably set up by Hazael king of Aram, celebrates victories over a king of Israel and a king of the “House of David”; 2 Kings 8:28–29 tells of wars against Hazael in the days of Joram and Ahaziah. The main debate is no longer whether a Davidic dynasty existed, but how far his kingdom reached.

Which details it helps explain

Israel Finkelstein’s low chronology lowers Iron Age dates by about a century and sees tenth-century BC Jerusalem as a village without monumental architecture. Between 2007 and 2013 Yosef Garfinkel and Saar Ganor excavated Khirbet Qeiyafa, about 30 km southwest of Jerusalem above the Elah Valley: a 2.3-hectare town with a casemate wall and two gates, which they identify with Shaaraim and link with a central authority in Judah. William Dever and Aaron Burke criticize the low chronology; other scholars accept the date of Qeiyafa but debate which polity it belonged to.

Which limit must remain

Inscriptions and sites show that David was a historical figure remembered as the founder of a dynasty, but they do not settle the size of his kingdom or of his capital.

What you can now recognize

What context contributes to the account of the City of David

The City of David is a real hill beside the Gihon Spring, and a ninth-century BC Aramaic inscription remembers the kings of the “House of David”. But specialists do not agree on how David entered the stronghold, whether his palace has been found or how large Jerusalem was in his time, so none of these proposals should be presented as proven.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he establishes David as king for the sake of his people and makes a conquered stronghold the place where he will set his name. Context illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

What does this add to my reading?

1. The king’s strength

According to 2 Samuel 5:10–12, to whom does the text attribute David’s growth, and what does he understand?

See a guided answer

The text says that David grew greater because the Lord God of Armies was with him. David perceives that God has established him as king and exalted his kingdom for the sake of Israel: the capital is not a personal trophy but a service to God’s people.

2. A house for David

In 2 Samuel 7:11–16 David wants to build a house for God. What house does God promise him?

Return to 2 Samuel 7:11–16
See a guided answer

God announces that he himself will build David a “house”, that is, a line of descendants, and that his throne will be established for ever. The very expression found on the Tel Dan Stele, “House of David”, is in Scripture first of all a promise from God.

Result of the journey: you can now recognize the central message of the account, place the City of David prudently on Jerusalem’s southeastern hill and explain fairly why the palace, the tunnel and the chronology are still debated.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Zion
Name of the Jebusite stronghold that David took (2 Samuel 5:7); later it designates the temple mount, all Jerusalem and the people of God.
Jebusites
The inhabitants of Jerusalem before David; 1 Chronicles 11:4 recalls that the city was then called Jebus.
Millo
A structure in the City of David mentioned in 2 Samuel 5:9, probably a fill or terrace; its archaeological identification is debated.
Tsinnor
Hebrew word in 2 Samuel 5:8, often translated “water shaft” or “channel”; it is rare and its exact meaning is debated.
High / low chronology
Two ways of dating the Iron Age strata; the low chronology places them about a century later and changes the picture of the tenth century BC.
