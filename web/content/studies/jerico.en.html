Jericho: the account and the archaeology

The book of Joshua tells how Rahab protects the spies and how the walls of Jericho fall. We will first observe the sacred text and then the site of Tell es-Sultan, the history of its excavations and the debate about the date of its destruction.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what the excavations at Jericho show and what remains an open question of dates.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Archaeology helps when three things are kept apart: the site that can be studied, the excavators’ interpretations and the event narrated by Scripture.

Widely recognized context

One of the oldest cities

Tell es-Sultan, beside the spring of Ain es-Sultan, preserves traces of human activity from around 10,500 BC and, in the Neolithic, a wall and a stone tower. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2023.

Widely recognized context

A walled city destroyed by fire

The excavations of Garstang, Kenyon and the Italian-Palestinian expedition documented a fortified Bronze Age city that ended in a violent destruction: bricks fallen at the foot of the wall, a thick burn layer and jars full of burnt grain.

Unproven identification

When did that destruction happen?

Kenyon and the Italian-Palestinian expedition place it at the end of the Middle Bronze Age, around 1550 BC; Garstang and later Bryant Wood date it to around 1400 BC on the basis of pottery and a carbon-14 sample. The date of the Exodus and the conquest is also debated (fifteenth or thirteenth century BC). The debate remains open.

Unproven identification

Jericho in the Late Bronze Age

Kenyon found few Late Bronze Age remains and concluded that there was no strongly fortified city at that time; the Italian-Palestinian expedition is studying a building from 1500–1200 BC. How large the population was and what defences existed in that period is unresolved.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and the site

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: victory as a gift and Rahab’s faith

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that the city is God’s gift before it is a human conquest: “I have given Jericho into your hand” (Joshua 6:2; 6:16). Israel does not take it with siege engines but by obeying and trusting.

Which details it helps explain

The procession with the ark and the trumpets, the silence and the shout (6:10, 20), Rahab’s confession (2:11) and her rescue. Hebrews 11:30–31 cites both the fall of the walls and Rahab as examples of faith, and James 2:25 praises her deed.

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the date of the destruction or the archaeological appearance of the city.

Related readings: source 2.

02

Tell es-Sultan: one of the oldest cities

What it contributes

It places the account in a concrete location: the mound of Tell es-Sultan, in the oasis of Jericho, beside a perennial spring that sustained life for millennia.

Which details it helps explain

Remains from around 10,500 BC; in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, a settlement of about 2.5 hectares with a wall and a stone tower some 8 metres high; in the Early and Middle Bronze Ages, a fortified Canaanite city with earthen ramparts. Inscribed as a World Heritage site in 2023.

Which limit must remain

The age of the site and its walls do not by themselves identify the city that fell in Joshua’s time: one has to ask which layer belongs to which period.

03

The excavations and the dating debate

What it contributes

It explains why specialists disagree. John Garstang (1930–1936) dated the fall of the city to around 1400 BC. Kathleen Kenyon (1952–1958) showed that the walls he had attributed to Joshua belonged to the Early Bronze Age and dated the destruction of the last fortified city to around 1550 BC. In 1990 Bryant Wood re-examined Garstang’s pottery and argued once more for a date around 1400 BC.

Which details it helps explain

Kenyon’s stratigraphic method, the painted pottery and a carbon-14 sample (1410 BC ± 40) cited by Wood, the fallen bricks and the grain jars; and the Italian-Palestinian expedition of Sapienza University of Rome and the Palestinian Department of Antiquities (since 1997, directed by Lorenzo Nigro), which dates the Middle Bronze destruction to around 1550 BC and is studying Late Bronze Age remains.

Which limit must remain

Neither date has been conclusively demonstrated: both depend on how the pottery, the layers and the biblical chronology are interpreted.

What you can now recognize

What archaeology contributes to the Jericho account

Tell es-Sultan is one of the oldest inhabited places in the world, and a walled Bronze Age city stood there that ended in destruction by fire. But the excavators do not agree on when that destruction happened or on what Jericho was like in the Late Bronze Age, so the ruins should not be presented either as proof or as a refutation of the account.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he gives the city, calls for trust and obedience, and saves a Canaanite woman who believed in him. Archaeology illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

Return to the sacred text

What does this add to my reading?

1. Rahab’s confession

According to Joshua 2:9–11, what does Rahab know about the Lord, and what leads her to protect the spies?

See a guided answer

Rahab has heard how the Lord dried up the Red Sea and defeated the Amorite kings, and she confesses that he is God in heaven above and on earth beneath. Her act of protection springs from that faith, which is why Hebrews 11:31 remembers her among the examples of faith.

2. Who gives the city?

In Joshua 6:15–20, to whom does the text attribute the fall of Jericho, and what part do the people play?

Return to Joshua 6:20
See a guided answer

Joshua says: “The Lord has given you the city” (6:16). The people walk, keep silent, shout and obey, but the walls fall without a siege or engines of war. Hebrews 11:30 sums it up: “By faith the walls of Jericho fell down.”

Key terms

Brief glossary

Tell
Artificial mound formed by the accumulated ruins of successive cities built on the same spot.
Ain es-Sultan
Perennial spring beside the mound of Jericho, which explains the occupation of the site over millennia.
Middle Bronze / Late Bronze Age
Archaeological periods of the second millennium BC; the transition from one to the other is placed around 1550 BC.
Stratigraphy
The study of a site’s layers to establish their order and relative date; Kenyon applied it rigorously at Jericho.
Devoted thing
What is consecrated to the Lord and excluded from the plunder (Joshua 6:17–19); in Hebrew, herem.
