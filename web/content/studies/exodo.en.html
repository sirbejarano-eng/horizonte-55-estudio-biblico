The Exodus: from the sea to Mount Sinai

Exodus tells of Israel’s departure from Egypt, the crossing of the sea and the arrival at the mountain of God. We will first observe the sacred text and then the Egypt of the Delta, the proposed routes and the debate about Sinai.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what the Egyptian context contributes and what remains a proposed location.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Ancient context helps when three things are kept apart: the Egypt that can be studied, the proposed routes and the event narrated by Scripture.

Widely recognized context

Rameses and the eastern Delta

Pi-Ramesses, the capital of Ramesses II (1279–1213 BC), is identified with the site of Qantir in the eastern Delta. Many link it with Rameses in Exodus 1:11.

Widely recognized context

Israel already exists as a people in Canaan

The stele of Pharaoh Merneptah, from the late thirteenth century BC, mentions “Israel” as a people in Canaan: the earliest commonly accepted reference outside the Bible.

Unproven identification

The date of the Exodus

1 Kings 6:1 places the departure 480 years before Solomon’s fourth year, which points to the fifteenth century BC; others place it in the thirteenth century because of the mention of Rameses. The debate remains open.

Unproven identification

The route, the sea and the mountain

Neither the exact place of the sea crossing nor that of Mount Sinai can be identified with certainty: there are several proposals and none is proven.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its context

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: deliverance and covenant

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that it is God who brings Israel out of Egypt, guides them and leads them to the mountain to make a covenant with them (Exodus 19:4–6; 20:2).

Which details it helps explain

The reason for the detour through the wilderness (13:17), the pillar of cloud and fire, and the people’s fear and faith after crossing the sea (14:31).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the date, the route or the location of the mountain.

Related readings: source 1.

02

The Egypt of the Delta and the date

What it contributes

It places the account in the eastern Delta, where Pi-Ramesses stood, and offers external reference points such as the Merneptah Stele.

Which details it helps explain

The cities of Pithom and Rameses (Exodus 1:11), Israel’s presence in Canaan in the late thirteenth century BC and the two proposed dates for the Exodus.

Which limit must remain

These data frame the account, but they do not date the departure precisely or replace the biblical chronology of 1 Kings 6:1.

Related readings: source 4 and source 5.

03

The sea and the mountain: proposed locations

What it contributes

It explains why there are several proposals. The Hebrew expression yam suf can be rendered “Sea of Reeds”, and the Bible also uses it for the Gulf of Aqaba (1 Kings 9:26). For the mountain, Jebel Musa has been the tradition since at least the fourth century; Jebel Serbal and sites in Arabia have also been proposed.

Which details it helps explain

The lakes of the Isthmus of Suez, the Gulfs of Suez and Aqaba; the pilgrim Egeria (around 381–384), Justinian’s monastery (sixth century) and the mention of “Mount Sinai in Arabia” (Galatians 4:25), which in Paul’s time could include the Sinai Peninsula.

Which limit must remain

An ancient tradition or a suitable landscape is not a proven identification: no proposal for the sea or the mountain has been demonstrated.

What you can now recognize

What context contributes to the Exodus account

The Egypt of the Ramesside pharaohs, with its capital in the eastern Delta, and Israel’s presence in Canaan at the end of the thirteenth century BC help us picture the setting of the account. But they do not allow us to trace the exact route or to point with certainty to the place of the sea or the mountain.

The centre of the account lies in what God does: he delivers, guides, opens the way and brings his people to the covenant. Context illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

1. Look at the detour

According to Exodus 13:17–18, why does God not lead the people by the shortest way?

The text gives the reason: so that the people would not change their minds when they saw war and return to Egypt. The route has a pastoral purpose first; its exact geographical course comes second.

2. Who opens the way?

In Exodus 14:21–31, to whom does the text attribute the opening of the sea, and how do the people respond?

Moses stretches out his hand, but it is the Lord who drives the wind and saves Israel (14:30). The account mentions a means — the east wind — while still presenting it as God’s work, and it ends with the people’s fear and faith (14:31).

Key terms

Brief glossary

Yam suf
Hebrew expression usually translated “Red Sea”, which can literally be understood as “Sea of Reeds”.
Rameses / Pi-Ramesses
City in the eastern Delta, capital of Ramesses II, identified with the site of Qantir.
Horeb
Another biblical name for the mountain of God (Exodus 3:1), identified with Sinai.
Jebel Musa
“Mountain of Moses” in the south of the Sinai Peninsula; the traditional location of the mountain, not a proven identification.
Merneptah Stele
Egyptian inscription from the late thirteenth century BC that mentions Israel as a people.
