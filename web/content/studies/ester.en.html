Esther in Susa: for such a time as this

The book of Esther tells how a young Jewish woman becomes queen of Persia and risks her life to save her people. We will first observe the sacred text and then the Susa of the Achaemenid kings, the court of Xerxes and the debate about the characters of the account.

By the end: you will be able to distinguish what the sacred text affirms, what Susa and the Persian court contribute and what remains open, such as the identity of Vashti, Esther and Mordecai outside the Bible.

Study criterion

Scripture is the foundation

Horizonte 55 receives the sacred text as the foundation of this study. Archaeology and history can illuminate the ancient setting, but they do not replace Scripture’s message or turn a possible association into a proven identification.

Degrees of certainty

What can be recognized and what remains open

Ancient context helps when three things are kept apart: the Susa that can be studied, the proposed identifications and the message the account puts at its centre.

Widely recognized context

Susa and the palace of Darius

Susa was an administrative capital of the Achaemenid Persian Empire. Darius I built a palace there with a great columned hall, the Apadana; today it is a UNESCO World Heritage site, and the Louvre holds the Frieze of Archers and a bull capital found there.

Widely recognized context

Xerxes I, king of Persia

Xerxes I, son and successor of Darius I, reigned from 486 to 465 BC, built at Susa and Persepolis and led the invasion of Greece (480 BC). The identification of Ahasuerus with Xerxes is the most widely accepted.

Unproven identification

Mordecai in the tablets?

A fifth-century BC cuneiform tablet mentions a scribe named Marduka who visited Susa, and the name also appears in Persepolis tablets. Some link him with Mordecai; it was a common name and the identification is not proven.

Unproven identification

Vashti, Esther and Herodotus’s queen

Neither Vashti nor Esther appears in the known Persian or Greek sources; Herodotus calls Xerxes’ wife Amestris. The historicity of several details of the book is still debated.

Three complementary lenses

How to read the account and its context

These three lenses do not compete with each other: they answer different questions and must keep different degrees of certainty.

01

Theological reading: a providence without a name

What it contributes

It brings to the fore that God, though unnamed, keeps watching over his people in exile: relief will come “from another place”, and Esther may have come to the kingdom “for such a time as this” (Esther 4:14).

Which details it helps explain

The contrast between the king’s apparent power (chapter 1) and the frailty of a young woman who fasts and risks her life (4:16); the “coincidences” of the account, such as the king’s sleepless night (6:1); and the feast of Purim, in which mourning is turned into joy (9:20–22).

Which limit must remain

The theological reading does not by itself fix the date of the events or identify the characters in outside sources.

Related readings: source 1.

02

Susa and the palace of the Persian kings

What it contributes

It gives visible shape to the setting of the book. Darius I made Susa one of the centres of the empire and built on a great terrace a palace and an Apadana of 72 columns some twenty metres high, with halls and courtyards like those the account mentions.

Which details it helps explain

The Frieze of Archers, in coloured glazed bricks, with archers on either side of a trilingual inscription of Darius; a stone capital with two bulls joined back to back; the excavations of Marcel Dieulafoy (1884–1886), now in the Louvre.

Which limit must remain

These finds show what Persian Susa was like, but they do not name Esther or Mordecai, nor do they prove the events of the account by themselves.

03

The court of Xerxes and its characters

What it contributes

It helps us recognize the Persian colouring of the book: royal banquets, eunuchs serving the king and queen (1:10), a council of nobles (1:13–14), the king’s ring to seal decrees and couriers on horseback carrying them to every province (3:12–13; 8:10), and Persian words such as “nobles” (1:3) or “pavilion” (1:5).

Which details it helps explain

Ahasuerus corresponds to the Persian name Khshayarsha, which the Greeks wrote as Xerxes. His third year (1:3) would fall a few years before the invasion of Greece. Carey Moore notes that the account reads like a carefully crafted narrative, although nothing in it is impossible; Michael Heltzer draws attention to the scribe Marduka who visited Susa.

Which limit must remain

A Persian setting does not settle whether every detail is historical: the identity of Vashti and Esther outside the Bible remains unconfirmed.

What you can now recognize

What context contributes to the account of Esther

The Susa of Darius and Xerxes, with its terrace, its Apadana and its glazed brick friezes, together with the vocabulary and customs of the Persian court, helps us picture the setting of the book. But it does not allow us to prove that Mordecai is the Marduka of the tablets or to find Vashti or Esther in the Persian sources.

The centre of the account lies in what God does without being named: he protects his people in a foreign land, works through Esther’s courage and turns mourning into feasting. Context illuminates the setting; the sacred text defines the message.

What does this add to my reading?

1. Look at the king’s power

The text piles up provinces, months of feasting, gold, silver and marble (1:1–8). Yet this king who seems to control everything cannot make the queen come at his call (1:12). The book opens by showing that human power, however great, is not what decides history.

2. Who knows?

In Esther 4:14, where does Mordecai expect deliverance to come from, and what does he ask of Esther?

Return to Esther 4:14
See a guided answer

Mordecai is sure that deliverance will come even if Esther keeps silent, “from another place”, without naming God. But that does not excuse her: he asks her to speak, because she may have come to the kingdom for that very time. Providence does not cancel responsibility; it calls her to act, and Esther answers with fasting and courage (4:16).

Result of the journey: you can now recognize the central message of the book, place it prudently in Persian Susa and avoid presenting a tablet, a palace or a known queen as proof of Esther and Mordecai.

Key terms

Brief glossary

Susa
Ancient city in south-western Iran, administrative capital of the Achaemenid Persian Empire; called Shushan in some translations.
Apadana
Great Persian audience hall supported by columns, like the one Darius I built at Susa.
Ahasuerus
Biblical name of a Persian king, widely identified with Xerxes I (486–465 BC).
Pur / Purim
“Pur” means “lot”: Haman cast lots to fix the day of the massacre; hence the name of the feast of Purim (Esther 3:7; 9:24–26).
Marduka
Babylonian name, derived from the god Marduk, that appears in Persian-period tablets; some compare it with Mordecai.
